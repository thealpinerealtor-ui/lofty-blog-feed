# Elk Camp and Closing Dates: What Hunting Season Does to a Flathead Calendar

Archery season opened September 5. A handful of backcountry districts — 150, 280, and 316 — are already into their general rifle season, weeks ahead of the statewide rifle opener on October 24. If you're buying or selling in the Flathead Valley right now, that's not background noise. For a real share of the people on both sides of a transaction, the next several weeks run on a hunting calendar as much as a closing calendar, whether their agent has noticed or not.

I hunt this valley myself, so this isn't a lifestyle piece written from the outside looking in. It's the same discipline I brought to scheduling movements and coordinating with people who weren't always reachable — applied to something lower-stakes, but with the same basic problem: if you build a plan assuming everyone's phone works and everyone's available "whenever," that plan breaks the first time it meets reality.

## Where This Shows Up First: Showings

A listing agent who sets a showing window without asking about hunting plans is going to eat a few no-shows this month, and more in October. It's not that buyers and sellers are being difficult — it's that "available anytime" quietly stops being true for a meaningful slice of the valley starting in September and doesn't fully reset until well into November.

The fix is simple and it's the same one I use for anything with a hard deadline: ask early, and ask specifically. Not "are you around this month," but "are there dates you're out of cell range, and dates you know you're back." A seller who tells you October 24–28 is elk camp isn't giving you a scheduling headache — they're giving you the information that keeps a showing from getting booked into a week that was never going to work.

## Where It Actually Bites: Inspections and Walkthroughs

Showings are an inconvenience if you get the timing wrong. Inspections and final walkthroughs are different — they sit on a contract with dates attached, and Montana purchase agreements don't pause for opening weekend.

If a buyer or seller is going to be in the backcountry during an inspection contingency window, that needs to be flagged before the offer is written, not discovered three days before the deadline. The same goes for a final walkthrough scheduled against a closing date — if the seller who needs to be present is going to be miles from a cell tower, that's a conversation for the purchase agreement's timeline, not a last-minute scramble with a title company waiting on a signature.

None of this is complicated. It just has to happen on purpose. I build every closing calendar around the assumption that at least one person involved — buyer, seller, sometimes both — has a week where they're genuinely unreachable, and I plan the contingency deadlines and walkthrough dates around that instead of hoping it doesn't come up.

## What a Realistic October Looks Like

By the time the statewide rifle opener hits on October 24, this stops being a background consideration and becomes the default assumption. Multiple-day gaps in availability. Weekends that are off the table months in advance. Sellers who want their home shown by appointment only, with real notice, because they're not going to drop everything for a same-day request during the two weeks that matter most to them all year.

None of that has to slow a transaction down. It just means the calendar gets built with those weeks already blocked out, instead of getting rebuilt around them after a missed deadline. A seller who tells me in September which weeks in October are off-limits gets a listing strategy and a showing plan that respects that from day one. A buyer who flags the same thing gets an offer timeline that doesn't assume they're glued to their phone during opening weekend.

## The Practical Version

If you're planning to list, buy, or are already under contract heading into archery or rifle season: say so, early, and say so specifically. Give dates, not "sometime in October." If you're working with an agent who hasn't asked, ask them how they plan around it — the answer tells you a lot about whether your calendar is going to hold up once the valley empties out for a few weeks.

This isn't about slowing anything down. It's about building the plan around the parts of your life that don't move for a closing date, instead of finding out the hard way that they don't.

Do you have dates this fall where you know you'll be out of range — and does your current plan already account for them, or is it going to find out the hard way?
