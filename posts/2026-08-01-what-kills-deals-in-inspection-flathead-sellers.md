# What Kills Deals in Inspection — and How Flathead Sellers Get Ahead of It

Here's a question most sellers never think to ask until it's too late: if a buyer's inspector found a problem with your home next week, would you rather find out now — while you control the timeline — or twelve days into a contract, when the buyer controls the leverage?

Because that's really what the inspection period is. It's a leverage transfer. The day you accept an offer, the buyer gains the right to reopen the negotiation you thought was finished — and the inspection report is the tool they use to do it.

Deals in the Flathead Valley rarely die at the negotiating table. They die in the crawl space, on the roof, and at the wellhead. The good news: almost every deal-killer on that list is something you can find, price, and neutralize before your home ever hits the market.

## The Valley's Usual Suspects

Every market has its inspection landmines. Ours are shaped by hard winters, rural systems, and how we heat our homes.

**Wells and septic systems.** A large share of homes outside Kalispell city limits — Bigfork, Lakeside, Somers, the rural stretches of Columbia Falls — run on private wells and septic. Buyers moving here from cities with municipal everything get nervous fast when a flow test comes back marginal or a septic inspection finds a tank that hasn't been pumped in a decade. A failed septic inspection isn't a $500 conversation. Replacement systems can run well into five figures, and that number lands on the negotiating table with a thud.

**Roofs after hard winters.** Snow load, ice dams, and freeze-thaw cycles age a roof faster here than the same shingles would age in milder climates. An inspector calling out curled shingles, damaged flashing, or signs of ice damming gives a buyer two options: ask for a new roof, or walk. Neither is a conversation you want to have under deadline pressure.

**Wood stoves and chimneys.** They're part of how the valley lives — and they're one of the most commonly flagged items in local inspections. Missing hearth clearances, unlined chimneys, stoves installed without permits decades ago. Insurance companies have tightened up on wood-burning appliances, which means a flagged stove can complicate the buyer's insurance, which can complicate their loan.

**Radon.** Montana sits in a high-radon region, and a meaningful share of Flathead Valley homes test above the EPA action level. The fix — a mitigation system — is usually a four-figure item, not a catastrophe. But a surprise radon result mid-contract spooks buyers out of proportion to the actual cost, because it sounds scarier than it is.

## Why Surprises Cost More Than Problems

Here's the part most sellers miss: the issue itself is rarely what kills the deal. The *surprise* is.

A buyer who learns about a $9,000 septic issue from their own inspector — mid-contract, from a stranger, in a report full of red flags — doesn't just see a repair cost. They start wondering what else you didn't know about. Trust erodes, and suddenly they're renegotiating everything or walking entirely.

A buyer who sees the same issue disclosed upfront, with a bid attached and the price already reflecting it? That buyer sees an honest seller and a known quantity. Same problem. Completely different deal.

In my previous line of work, we had a saying about exactly this: bad news doesn't get better with time. The earlier you surface a problem, the more options you have for dealing with it. Wait, and the problem starts dictating terms to you.

## The Pre-List Inspection: Taking Back the Timeline

This is why I talk with most of my listing clients about a pre-list inspection — hiring your own inspector *before* the sign goes in the yard, typically for a few hundred dollars.

What it buys you is control, in three specific ways:

**You repair on your schedule and your budget.** Fixing a flashing issue in the calm before listing means getting competitive bids and choosing your contractor. Fixing it under a contract deadline means paying whatever the one available contractor charges — or crediting the buyer an inflated estimate for the same work.

**You price with real information.** If the roof has five years left, you can decide: replace it and price accordingly, or price it in and disclose. Either works. What doesn't work is pricing as if the roof is fine and discovering otherwise in week two of a contract.

**You take the report's power away.** When the buyer's inspection confirms what you already disclosed, there's nothing to renegotiate. The scary document becomes a formality. I've watched deals in Kalispell and Whitefish close smoothly over issues that would have been fatal as surprises — because the seller got there first.

## What This Looks Like in Practice

The sequence is simple. Inspect before you list. Fix what's worth fixing — usually the cheap, high-alarm items like handrails, GFCI outlets, and stove clearances. Get bids on the bigger items so you can attach real numbers to disclosures instead of leaving buyers to imagine worst cases. Then price the home with everything on the table.

One note on disclosure: once you know about a material defect, Montana law expects you to disclose it. Some sellers hear that and decide they'd rather not know. I'd push back on that logic — the buyer's inspector is going to find it anyway. The only question is whether it gets found while you control the response or while they do. And as always, for questions about your specific disclosure obligations, consult your attorney.

If you're thinking about selling this fall, ask yourself honestly: what would a good inspector find at your place? Most homeowners already know — the thing they've been meaning to deal with, the noise the furnace makes, the corner of the roof they don't look at.
