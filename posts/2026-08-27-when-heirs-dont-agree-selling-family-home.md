# When the Heirs Don't Agree: Selling a Family Home Together

The hardest estate sales I've been part of in the Flathead Valley weren't hard for legal reasons. The paperwork was clean. The title was clear. The probate was routine.

They were hard because three siblings wanted three different things, and one of them was living in the house.

If that's the situation you're in — or the one you can feel coming — here's where these go well, and where they go sideways.

First, the disclaimer, and I mean it: I'm a real estate agent, not an attorney. Montana probate has rules about authority, notice, and what a personal representative may do without court involvement, and those rules depend on the will and the specifics of the estate. What follows is meant to help you ask your attorney sharper questions, not to replace the answer.

## Name what each person actually wants

Before anyone argues about price, get clear that the disagreement usually isn't about the house at all.

One heir wants the money, often on a timeline. One wants to keep the place in the family — it's where Thanksgiving happened. One is exhausted and wants it over with. And sometimes one is living there, has been for years, and is quietly afraid of being told to leave a home they've been maintaining.

That's four different problems getting treated as one argument about a listing price, which is exactly why the argument never resolves. Put the wants on the table plainly — money, memory, speed, shelter — and stop pretending everyone is negotiating the same point. You can build a plan around four stated needs. You can't build one around a fight.

## What the personal representative can and can't decide alone

This question produces the most tension and the most misinformation.

In broad terms, the personal representative carries a legal duty to the estate and to *all* the beneficiaries — not to whichever heir calls most often. That duty runs both directions: it gives the PR real authority to act, and it obligates them to act in the estate's interest rather than their own.

What that authority covers in practice — whether the home can be listed without every heir signing, what notice the other beneficiaries are owed, whether court approval is required — depends on the will, the type of probate, and the facts of the estate. Ask your attorney to spell it out in writing, early. A PR who knows the boundary of their own authority decides calmly. One who doesn't tends to either overstep or freeze, and both create resentment that outlasts the sale.

That duty also means preserving the estate's value. A house sitting empty through a Flathead winter, unheated and underinsured, isn't a neutral choice.

## An independent valuation ends more arguments than any conversation

Here's the single most effective de-escalation tool in an estate sale: get a number from someone who has nothing to gain.

When value is a matter of opinion, every opinion is suspect. The sibling who wants to sell is accused of lowballing. The one who wants to buy the others out is accused of grabbing a bargain. The one out of state has a Zestimate and a theory. Nobody trusts anyone.

A licensed appraisal — often the date-of-death appraisal the estate needs for tax purposes anyway — replaces all of that with a document. Appraised value and market value answer slightly different questions, so it isn't the last word. But it's neutral, it's defensible, and it moves the conversation from *"you're trying to cheat me"* to *"here's the number — now what do we do about it."*

Get it early, before positions harden. It costs a few hundred dollars and routinely saves families five figures in legal fees, plus something more expensive than that.

## When one heir wants to buy the others out

This is common, and often the right outcome. It usually fails for one of three reasons.

**The number was never independently set.** A buyout priced by argument is a buyout that gets relitigated later.

**Financing wasn't confirmed first.** An heir who intends to buy the others out needs to know what they can actually qualify for, and lenders treat estate and probate situations with their own requirements. Answer that in week one, not in month five after everyone has emotionally committed.

**Nobody wrote down a deadline.** The family that agrees "he'll buy us out when he can" is the family still arguing two years later.

A workable buyout has three things in writing: an independent value, a confirmed source of funds, and a date by which it closes or the house goes to market. Have your attorney paper it.

## When someone simply won't sign

Sometimes it isn't obstruction. It's grief wearing the costume of obstruction — the person who won't sign is often the one who isn't ready to close the door on their parents' house, and pressure doesn't fix that.

Slow down enough to find out which one it is. If it's grief, a date on the calendar a few weeks out does more than an ultimatum. If it's a genuine deadlock, that's where your attorney explains the options the estate actually has — worth understanding before anyone makes a threat they'd rather not carry out.

What I'd guard against is letting a deadlock run indefinitely. Every month the estate carries a house nobody is using, it pays for the privilege — taxes, insurance, utilities, upkeep — out of the same pot everyone is arguing over.

## Set the timeline before you need it

Almost every estate sale I've seen go smoothly had one thing in common: someone wrote down the phases early and shared them with everyone. Valuation by this date. Buyout decision by this one. Contents cleared by this one, with a stated process for who claims what. On market by this date if the buyout hasn't closed. Nothing dramatic — just a plan, visible to all parties, so nobody has to guess what happens next.

That's the piece I bring to these. In the Army and in the work that followed, the plan wasn't optional and the briefing wasn't a formality — everyone knew the phases, the roles, and what triggered the next move. Families in the middle of a loss don't need a salesperson. They need someone who holds the timeline steady while they handle the harder part.

So here's the question worth sitting with: is your family actually disagreeing about the house — or about four different things nobody has said out loud yet?
