Would you rather find out about your roof now, or three days into a buyer's inspection period?

Same roof. Same shingles. Same amount of life left in them. But those are two completely different conversations, and the difference has almost nothing to do with the roof.

In the first one, you have time. You can get three bids. You can decide the repair isn't worth making and adjust your number instead. You can do nothing at all and say so up front.

In the second one, you're under contract. There's a repair request already drafted, a deadline attached to it, and a buyer who was handed a forty-page document full of red text this morning. Whatever you decide now, you're deciding it fast, with the leverage sitting across the table.

Most sellers in Kalispell and Whitefish end up in the second conversation — not because they were hiding anything, but because nobody suggested the first one.

## The Arithmetic Most Sellers Never Run

A pre-listing inspection runs a few hundred dollars for a standard single-family home in the valley. More if the house is large, if there are outbuildings, or if you add a separate well and septic evaluation — which, out on acreage here, you generally should.

Now price the alternative.

A mid-transaction repair request is rarely a bill for the actual repair. It's a bill for the repair plus the buyer's uncertainty. Someone reading "roof at end of service life" with no bid attached doesn't imagine the modest fix. They imagine the whole roof — and ask for the whole roof, because their agent has no reason to ask for less.

Then there's the contractor problem, which is specific to where we live. Getting a roofer out on your own timeline in April is a phone call. Getting one inside a ten-day response window in November, when everybody is trying to beat the weather, means taking whoever is available at whatever they quote. You're shopping without the ability to walk away.

Set a few hundred dollars against those two and it stops being a close call.

## Triage: Fix It, Price It In, or Disclose and Move On

Here's where people get it backwards. They order the inspection, open the report, count thirty-one flagged items, and panic.

Thirty-one items is a normal report. On a 1998 house with a crawlspace, it's a good one. Inspectors flag everything they see, including things that are simply how the house was built. The report isn't a to-do list — it's raw intelligence, and it needs sorting into three piles.

**Fix it.** Cheap, fast, and disproportionately alarming to read. Missing GFCI outlets. A loose handrail. A dryer vent running into the crawlspace. Improper clearance around the wood stove. None is expensive, and every one makes a buyer's inspector write a sentence that sounds worse than the problem is. You're not buying the repair — you're buying the absence of the sentence.

**Price it in.** Real money, real remaining life, no emergency. The roof with five or six years left. The furnace that works fine and is nineteen years old. The septic that passed but is at the age where a buyer starts doing math. You don't have to replace these — you have to be honest about them and let your number reflect them, with a written bid attached so the buyer reacts to a real figure instead of an imagined one.

**Disclose and move on.** Conditions that are permanent or simply characteristic of the house. The slope of the lot. The original single-pane window in the stairwell. The crawlspace you can't stand up in. These aren't problems to solve. They're facts about the property, and the right buyer accepts them.

The skill isn't in the fixing. It's in the sorting.

## Why Fixing Everything Is the Wrong Call

Sellers who try to clear all thirty-one items usually spend more than the market gives back, and often spend it in the wrong places.

Buyers here don't pay a premium for a house with no deficiencies. They pay for the house — the light, the land, the view of the Swan Range, the shop, the fact that it's eleven minutes from the resort. A spotless report doesn't move the number much. A bad surprise costs you leverage at the moment you have the least of it.

There's a sequencing reason too. Every dollar you spend before listing is a dollar you can't put toward a price adjustment later if the market asks for one.

## Once You Know, You Know

A fair objection comes up here: if I don't inspect, I don't know, and if I don't know, I don't have to disclose it.

Montana expects sellers to disclose material defects they're aware of, and your broker carries obligations of their own. But the instinct doesn't survive contact with reality, because the buyer is hiring an inspector regardless. The defect gets found either way. The only variable is whether it's found while you control the response or while they do.

Willful ignorance buys you nothing but a worse version of the same day. Confirm your specific disclosure obligations with your broker and your attorney — that's their call, not mine.

## The Report Becomes the Argument

Used well, the report stops being a liability and turns into an asset. When you hand a serious buyer a report you commissioned, with the small items already corrected and written bids attached to the larger ones, you've changed what you're selling. You're not asking them to trust the house is sound. You're showing them. A buyer who isn't worried about what's behind the walls negotiates on price alone — a negotiation you can prepare for.

Their inspector will still come, as they should. But when their report says what yours already said, there's nothing to renegotiate.

## The Question Worth Sitting With

If you listed this month and a buyer's inspector walked your house on the fifteenth, what would he find that you'd rather have known about in advance?

Most sellers can name it immediately. There's usually one thing — the crawlspace, the roof, the panel, the drainage on the north side — that's been in the back of your mind for a year or two. That instinct is almost always right, and it's the cheapest thing in the process to check.
