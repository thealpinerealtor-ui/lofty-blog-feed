# Radio Discipline: Why Clients Get a Weekly Update, Not Just the Big News

There's a habit that gets trained into you early in high-stakes work, and it has nothing to do with real estate on its face: you report in on schedule, whether or not you have anything to say.

In the kind of work I came from, silence on the radio was never read as good news. It was read as the opposite — as a break in contact, a gap nobody could account for. The whole point of a scheduled check-in wasn't to deliver big news. Most of the time there wasn't any. The point was that the absence of an update, on schedule, is itself information, and it's the kind of information that makes people start assuming the worst. So you report in anyway. "Nothing to report" is a complete sentence, and it's a useful one.

I still run that way. Every client of mine gets a standing weekly update — a real one, not a form email — whether or not there's a showing, an offer, or a document to sign that week. Here's why that's a rule and not a nicety.

## What the Three-Week Silence Actually Does

Picture a listing that's been on the market five weeks. Showings were steady the first two, then they slowed, the way they do. Nothing dramatic has happened. Nobody's called with news because there isn't any news — no offer, no feedback worth flagging, no change in strategy.

To the agent, that's an ordinary week. To the seller sitting at home watching the listing count days on Zillow, three weeks without a word from their agent doesn't read as "nothing happening." It reads as "nobody's paying attention to my house anymore." That gap gets filled with a worse story than the truth almost every time — the agent's lost interest, the price is wrong and nobody wants to say it, the market's dead and nobody's telling me. None of that has to be true for the seller to start believing it. The silence does the work on its own.

The same thing happens on the buy side, especially during a financing contingency or an inspection response that's taking longer than expected because the other side is slow, not because anything is wrong. A buyer who hears nothing for two weeks in the middle of that stretch isn't reassured by the quiet. They're bracing for bad news that hasn't arrived yet and might never arrive at all.

## The Update That Says Nothing, on Purpose

A weekly check-in that just says "still nothing new, here's what we're watching for next" sounds almost too simple to matter. It matters anyway, for a reason that has nothing to do with the content of the message and everything to do with the fact that it showed up on schedule.

What it actually does: it moves the responsibility for staying informed off the client and onto the process. Nobody has to wonder whether to call and ask "is everything okay?" — because the answer arrives before the question does. It also does something quieter: it establishes, week over week, that if something did go wrong, the client would hear about it the same way, on the same rhythm, without having to chase it down. That's the trust that actually gets tested — not in the easy weeks, but in the one where there's a real problem to report, because by then the client already knows what "Ryan tells me things" looks like in practice.

## What This Isn't

It isn't a template email with a mail-merge field for the address. It isn't manufacturing false urgency to look busy, and it isn't padding a report with activity that didn't actually happen — a seller can tell the difference between "here's what actually occurred" and filler, and filler erodes the same trust it's trying to build. The discipline is specifically about showing up on the schedule with the truth, even when the truth is "quiet week, here's what's next," not about generating noise.

It also isn't reserved for the complicated files. Every client gets it — the straightforward resale closing in three weeks and the estate sale working through probate and the divorce sale where both parties are reading the same update on the same day so neither one wonders what the other one was told. The standard doesn't flex based on how easy the file is. That's what makes it a standard instead of a courtesy.

## The Actual Point

None of this is about being available around the clock, though the office line is answered that way. It's about a client never having to wonder, mid-week, whether anyone's still working on their behalf. The update on schedule is the answer to that question before it gets asked — which, in the stretches that feel the longest, from a divorce timeline to a slow month on the market, is usually the thing that matters most.

If you're working with someone right now and you can't remember the last time you heard from them without reaching out first, that's worth paying attention to — whether or not there's "big news" to report.
