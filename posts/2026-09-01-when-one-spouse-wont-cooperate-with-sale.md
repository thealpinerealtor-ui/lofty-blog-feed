# When One Spouse Won't Cooperate with the Sale

The hardest calls I take in this work don't start with a question about price.

They start with some version of this: *the decision to sell has already been made — by agreement, by attorneys, or by a judge — and one person has simply stopped participating.* Won't sign the listing paperwork. Won't confirm showing times. Won't leave the house for the open house. Answers the attorney but not the agent, or answers neither.

If that's where you are, the first thing worth saying is that this is more common than you'd guess, and it is almost never as unsolvable as it feels at week three.

## Non-cooperation usually isn't strategy

It's tempting to read silence as a tactic. Sometimes it is. Far more often it's one of three other things.

**It's grief with a deadline attached.** For one spouse, listing the house is paperwork. For the other, it's the last physical thing they're being asked to let go of, and every showing is a stranger walking through it. Slowing it down is the only lever left.

**It's an information gap.** People who haven't seen the numbers assume the worst about them. Someone who genuinely believes they're being rushed into a bad sale will dig in, and from inside that belief, digging in is the reasonable response.

**It's a control question, not a house question.** When most of a life has been decided by other people over the last year, the calendar of a home sale can become the one thing left to say no to.

None of that excuses a stalled sale. But the response to each is different, and treating all three as bad faith guarantees you get bad faith.

## Why your agent has to stay neutral — and what that buys you

If you're the spouse who wants this done, there's a version of this you might want from me: an advocate who works around the other party.

I won't do that, and the reason is practical rather than principled. The moment one spouse decides the agent belongs to the other one, every request I make becomes something to resist. A listing agent perceived as taking sides stops being able to move the transaction at all.

So the protocol is the same in every one of these files. Both parties get the same information at the same time, in the same message. Both attorneys are copied on anything substantive. No side conversations about strategy with either spouse, and I say so out loud to both of you at the start. Price opinions, net sheets, offer summaries, inspection findings — one document, one send, both names on it.

That's not fence-sitting. It's the thing that keeps a reluctant party at the table long enough to sign.

## Write the access rules into the listing agreement up front

Most cooperation fights are really scheduling fights, and most scheduling fights are avoidable if the rules get set before the sign goes in the yard.

When one spouse still occupies the home, we put the specifics in writing at listing:

- **Showing windows.** Defined blocks — weekday evenings and weekend afternoons, say — rather than open-ended access. A predictable schedule is far easier to agree to than "whenever a buyer asks."
- **Notice required, and how showings get confirmed.** A set minimum, one system, both parties notified — so there's never a dispute about whether a request was received.
- **Open house terms.** Whether there will be any, and who is responsible for being elsewhere.
- **Photography and marketing.** When photos happen and whether personal items are removed first.
- **Occupancy standard.** What condition the home is kept in, and who handles it.

Spelling this out at the start converts a series of individual asks — each of which can be refused — into one agreement already made. When a request is later declined, there's an objective standard to point at instead of a personality conflict.

## What a Montana court can do — and what your attorney drives

This is the part where I stop and say clearly: I'm not an attorney, nothing here is legal advice, and what gets filed in your case is your attorney's call, not mine.

What I can tell you as the person who works these files is that a district court handling a Montana dissolution has broad authority over the marital estate, including the home. Courts can and do enter orders addressing the sale of a marital residence — how it's listed, that a party sign the necessary documents, that showings be permitted, and in some cases appointing someone to sign on a refusing party's behalf so a closing isn't held hostage.

Whether any of that fits your situation, and whether it's worth the cost and the relationship damage of pursuing, is a conversation for your attorney. Most of the time it doesn't come to that. But it matters that you know the process isn't powerless — because the belief that it *is* powerless is what makes people accept a stall that's costing them real money.

## Document everything, in writing, without exception

If cooperation has broken down, this is the single most useful habit you can adopt today.

Every showing request, every proposed time, every response, every non-response — in writing. Not a message you'll paraphrase later. Not a phone call you remember clearly. Written, dated, and preserved.

Two reasons. First, if your attorney ever does need to show a court a pattern, a clean record of twenty specific declined or ignored requests is a different document than a summary of how the last two months felt.

Second, and more useful day to day: it protects *you*. Both spouses in a stalled sale usually believe the other one is the obstacle. A written record is the only thing that settles that without a fight — and it's just as capable of showing you were the difficult one as showing you weren't. That's what makes it credible.

On my side, requests go out in writing to both parties and both attorneys, and I keep the timeline. Not to build a case against anyone — because a documented process is a calm process, and calm is the whole job here.

## A question worth sitting with

If the sale closed sixty days from now, what would you want the record of these last few weeks to show about how you handled it?

That's usually the more useful question than who's being unreasonable — and it's the one you actually control.
