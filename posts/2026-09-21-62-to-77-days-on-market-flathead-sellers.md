Sixty-two. Seventy-seven.

Those are Kalispell's median days on market a year ago and today, respectively (source: Movoto Kalispell, MT market data, period through August 2026, page updated September 21, 2026). Fifteen extra days doesn't sound dramatic until you sit with what actually happens inside those two weeks — and it's worth sitting with, because a lot of sellers on the sidelines right now are measuring the wrong number entirely.

## The Numbers First

Kalispell, current snapshot: median sale price $699,000, 693 active listings, median days on market 77 — up from 62 a year ago (source: Movoto, data through August 2026, updated September 21, 2026).

On rate, the Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey put the 30-year fixed at 6.95% for the week of September 17, 2026, up from 6.76% the week before (source: Freddie Mac PMMS).

That's the whole picture in two sentences. Everything below is what a seller actually does with it.

## What "Two Extra Weeks" Really Means

A house that used to find a buyer in about nine weeks now takes closer to eleven. On paper that reads like a rounding error. In practice, those fifteen days are rarely quiet ones.

They're the window where a listing crosses from "new and interesting" to "still here" in a buyer's saved-search feed. They're the stretch where an agent previewing for a client starts asking why it hasn't sold yet, before ever asking what it's worth. And more often than not, they're the days where a price reduction gets discussed — not because the house changed, but because the calendar did.

Fifteen days by itself won't sink a listing. What sinks a listing is a seller who doesn't know those fifteen days are now the baseline, and who reads every one of them as evidence something is wrong with the house instead of evidence the whole market shifted underneath it.

## The Mistake: Measuring Against Last Year

The seller who gets hurt in this market isn't the one who's slow to sell. It's the one comparing this listing to a house that sold in June of last year, in nine weeks, at full price — and quietly deciding their own house is underperforming when it's actually tracking right at this year's median.

That comparison is the trap. Sixty-two days was last year's market. Seventy-seven is this one. A house sitting at day 60 today isn't behind schedule. It's roughly on pace. The seller who doesn't know that will start second-guessing the price around week seven, right when patience was the correct answer.

The fix isn't complicated: know the current median before you list, not the one you remember from a friend's sale eighteen months ago.

## What a Longer Market Actually Changes

A slower median doesn't just mean more waiting. It changes the mechanics of the deal itself, in three specific ways worth planning for before you're in the middle of them:

**More price reductions happen before contract, not after listing.** With more inventory for buyers to compare against, a house that opens a touch high has more competition sitting right next to it on the same search page. The reduction that used to be rare is now closer to routine — plan for the possibility rather than treating it as a failure if it happens.

**Appraisal-gap language gets softer.** When homes were moving in nine weeks, buyers waived contingencies to win multiple-offer situations. At 77 days, multiple offers are less common, and buyers have more room to ask for an appraisal contingency rather than bridging a gap in cash. Sellers should expect more negotiation on that clause, not less.

**Buyers have more to compare you against.** Six hundred ninety-three active listings means a buyer touring your house toured, or at least screened, several others this week. The house that shows clean, prices realistically against current comparables, and photographs well in the light it actually has is the one that doesn't need the full 77 days to find its buyer.

## What This Means for You This Week

If you're already on the market and past day 60, don't panic — check your pace against 77, the current number, not 62. If you're inside that window, you're not behind. If you're well past it, that's the moment for an honest look at price against what's actually closed nearby in the last 30 days, not what's still asking.

If you're waiting for spring hoping the market "comes back" to last year's pace, ask yourself what specifically you think changes between now and April. Rates aren't the lever — they've moved up, not down, over the past two weeks, and a rate drop would bring more buyers into every listing at once, not just yours. The seller who prices correctly and lists into a quieter fall often does better than the one who waits for a crowded spring.

So here's the actual question worth asking this week: do you know what your house is worth against what's closed in the last 30 days — not what's currently asking, what closed — or are you still pricing off a market that expired sometime last year?
