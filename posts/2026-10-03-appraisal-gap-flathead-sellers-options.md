# When the Appraisal Comes in Low: A Flathead Seller's Real Options

You've got a signed contract. The buyer is financing, the inspection went fine, and then the lender's appraisal comes back under your agreed price. Before you assume the deal is dead or that you have to eat the difference, here's what's actually happening and what you can do about it.

## Why this happens more often in a market like ours

An appraisal isn't an opinion of what a buyer is willing to pay — it's a lender's check that the collateral supports the loan, built off recent closed comparable sales. In a Flathead market where days on market have stretched out and price cuts have become more common on listings that sat too long, the comps an appraiser pulls can lag behind what an eager buyer just agreed to pay, especially on a property with real character: lake frontage, acreage, a custom build, or anything else that doesn't have a clean twin that closed in the last six months. The gap isn't usually a sign you overpriced the home. It's often a sign the appraisal process is backward-looking in a market that's still finding its footing.

## What the contract actually says happens next

Montana purchase contracts typically include a financing contingency, and inside that, an appraisal condition that gives the buyer an out if the home doesn't appraise at or above the contract price. That contingency protects the buyer, not you — which is exactly why sellers who don't read it closely get caught off guard. It does not automatically cancel the deal, and it does not automatically mean you have to lower your price. It means the buyer now has a decision to make, and so do you.

## Your four real options

**Challenge the appraisal.** This is the first move, and it's underused. Your agent can submit a reconsideration of value with better comps — recent closings the appraiser missed, pending sales that support the number, or documentation on upgrades and unique features that a drive-by comparison undervalues. This works more often than sellers expect, particularly on properties with acreage, outbuildings, or finishes that are hard to quantify from the street.

**Split the difference.** If the gap is modest, meeting the buyer partway on price is often the fastest path to closing without either side feeling like they lost. This is a negotiation, not a concession — know your number before you're in the conversation, not during it.

**Ask the buyer to bring the difference in cash.** Some buyers can and will cover the gap between the appraised value and the contract price out of pocket to keep the deal and the lower rate they locked. It's a reasonable ask, especially if the buyer has shown strong motivation throughout the transaction. It's not guaranteed, but it costs you nothing to put on the table.

**Let the contingency do its job.** If the numbers genuinely don't work for either side, the appraisal contingency allows the buyer to walk and get their earnest money back. That's a real outcome, and sometimes the right one — but it's worth knowing it's the last option, not the first, before anyone defaults to it.

## What a seller should not do

Don't treat a low appraisal as proof your asking price was wrong and reflexively drop it to match. An appraisal is one data point from one appraiser working off one set of comps on one day. It is not the market's final word, and plenty of homes that appraised low on a first pass have closed at or near the original contract price once the value got challenged or the buyer found a way to bridge the gap. Don't skip reading your own contract's appraisal language either — knowing your exact rights and deadlines before the number comes in gives you room to respond instead of react.

## The move that actually protects you

Have this conversation with your agent before you're under contract, not after an appraisal surprises you. Know going in what comps support your price, what your walk-away number is if a buyer asks you to come down, and whether the buyer's lender has any flexibility built in. A seller who's thought through the appraisal scenario in advance negotiates from a position of clarity. A seller who's hearing about it for the first time from a panicked phone call is negotiating from behind.

## A question worth sitting with

If your home went under contract today, do you know which comps your agent would use to challenge a low number — or would you be starting from zero the moment the appraisal landed? That's a five-minute conversation now that can save you a very stressful week later.
