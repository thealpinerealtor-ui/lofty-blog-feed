# Showings While You're Both Still Living There

The house is listed. Neither of you has moved out yet.

That's the month nobody plans for when a Flathead Valley divorce sale gets underway. Everyone braces for the closing table. Almost nobody braces for Tuesday at 4pm, when a buyer's agent wants to walk through the kitchen and both names on the deed are still sleeping under the same roof.

If you're wondering how other people get through this part without it becoming the worst month of the whole process, here's what actually works.

## One calendar, not two phones

The single biggest mistake I see is treating showings like any other logistics question between two people who are separating — handled by text, in the moment, back and forth.

That works fine for deciding who picks up milk. It does not work for a stream of showing requests coming in from agents who don't know your situation and don't need to. Every request that lands in a group text becomes a small negotiation, and a house going through a divorce sale generates a lot of small negotiations before it generates a closing.

The fix is boring on purpose: one written calendar, one place, both of you can see it without asking the other person anything. I set this up before the sign goes in the yard, not after the first awkward showing. Requests come to me, I check it against the calendar, I confirm or propose an alternate time, and both of you get the same notice at the same time. Neither of you is coordinating with the other directly, and neither of you is finding out about a showing from a lockbox log.

## Confirmed appointments only, and why that lowers the temperature

A house that's simply "available anytime" sounds convenient. In a situation like this, it's the opposite — it means either party can be caught off guard, which turns every doorbell into a small conflict.

Confirmed-appointments-only changes that. A showing isn't a showing until it's on the calendar and both parties have had the agreed notice window to prepare. No surprise walk-throughs, no "the agent said it would just be five minutes." That one rule removes most of the friction before it starts, because nobody is ever wondering whether someone might show up unannounced.

## The kids' schedule sets the boundaries, not the buyer's

Buyers' schedules are flexible. A ten-year-old's school pickup is not. I build the showing windows around the school run and activities first, and everything else gets scheduled inside what's left — evenings after the bus, weekend blocks that don't collide with practices or the other parent's time. A buyer's agent asking for a Tuesday-at-3pm slot doesn't get to override that just because it's the only opening in their calendar; there's almost always another time that works, and it's my job to find it rather than yours.

## Securing what shouldn't be part of the showing

A house that's occupied by two people mid-divorce usually has things sitting out that have nothing to do with selling it — financial documents, mail, medication, jewelry, anything either of you wouldn't want a stranger picking up off a dresser. Strangers walking through your home is already uncomfortable. It shouldn't also be a security risk.

Before the first showing, we walk the house together — or separately, whichever you prefer — and agree on where personal papers and valuables get secured for the duration of the listing. A lockable filing box in a closet, not spread across a desk. This is a five-minute conversation up front that saves a much worse one later.

## Whose job it is to have the house ready

This is the question that causes the most friction, and it's worth answering explicitly rather than assuming. If one of you is home more, or one of you cares more about how the house shows, that's fine — but it should be agreed, not defaulted into. I put it in writing at listing: who's responsible for the house being presentable on a scheduled showing day, and what "presentable" means, so it isn't relitigated every week.

## When one person starts declining showings

Sometimes a person who agreed to all of this at the start starts saying no to individual requests a few weeks in. Before assuming that's bad faith, it's worth asking why — a bad week, a scheduling conflict, or genuine discomfort with the pace. My job stays the same either way: keep both attorneys and both of you informed, document every request and every response in writing, and let that record speak for itself if it ever needs to. A documented process is a calm process, and staying neutral is what lets me keep working the file for both of you.

## A question worth sitting with

If a buyer's agent called right now asking for a showing tomorrow at 5pm, would you know — without a single text to your ex — whether that works?

If the answer is no, that's the gap worth closing before the first showing request comes in, not after.
