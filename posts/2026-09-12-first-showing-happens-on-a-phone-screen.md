How many buyers looked at your house this week and decided against it without you ever knowing they were there?

If the house is listed, the answer is dozens. Maybe hundreds. They looked at it on a phone — in a parking lot, in bed at eleven at night, on a couch in Spokane. Two to six seconds each. Then they kept scrolling.

That was the showing. The one with the lockbox and the shoes off at the door is the second showing, and few people ever get that far.

Sellers in Kalispell and Whitefish spend six weeks on the house and about four minutes on the media that represents it. It's backwards, and it's the most expensive inversion in the process.

## The Photos Aren't a Record of the House. They Are the House.

Until a buyer is standing in your kitchen, the twenty-odd images in the listing are not a depiction of your property. They are your property. Every judgment gets made from them — price, condition, whether the floor plan works, whether it's worth the drive from Missoula on a Saturday.

This matters more here than in a lot of markets, because a meaningful share of Flathead buyers are not in the Flathead. They're relocating, buying a second home, or watching from two states away. They'll build a shortlist of four houses from photos alone and fly in for one weekend. You are either on that list or you aren't, and nothing about your granite or your new well pump enters into it.

## Shoot Day Is the Real Deadline

Sellers set their sights on the list date. The date that matters is three or four days earlier, when the camera comes.

Everything has to be finished by then — not started, finished. The mulch down. The cars out of the driveway and off the street. The garbage cans gone, not tucked around the corner where the wide angle finds them anyway. Every light working, and every bulb the same color temperature — which sounds fussy until you see one room lit by three different whites.

You can keep improving the house after shoot day. The listing won't know. Whatever the camera sees is what the market sees for the whole run, and re-shoots almost never happen — by the time everyone agrees the photos are the problem, the listing is thirty days old and carrying that number around.

## Wide Angle, and the Point Where It Becomes a Lie

There's a real line here and it's worth naming.

A properly wide lens is honest work. Rooms photograph smaller than they feel, and correcting for that is just competence. Push it further and you get the listing everyone in this valley has seen: a bedroom that appears to run forty feet, walls that bow at the edges, a bathroom where the vanity is somehow half a mile from the tub.

Those photos do get showings. Then the buyer walks in, feels the gap, and spends the rest of the tour wondering what else was oversold. You didn't gain a showing — you spent one, and some credibility with it.

The same applies to the twilight shot and the drone. Both earn their place — twilight for a house whose best feature is how it sits at dusk with the lights on, drone for anything where the land or the setting is a real part of the value. Twenty acres above the valley floor earns a drone shot. A quarter-acre in-town lot does not, and using one there mostly tells a buyer you're reaching.

## What Fall Light Is Worth in January

It's mid-September. The grass is still green and the light is long and warm in the late afternoon. This is close to the best the valley photographs all year.

If there's any chance your house is still on the market in December, this is the argument for shooting now regardless of when you list. A house photographed in September and listed in November looks alive. The same house photographed in November looks like a house nobody wanted, which is a story the pictures tell whether it's true or not.

## Floor Plans Cut the Showings You Don't Want

A simple measured floor plan is cheap, usually bundled with the shoot, and does what photos can't: it answers the layout question.

Buyers are quietly solving problems — will the piano fit, can the mother-in-law have a room on the main floor, is the fourth bedroom usable or a closet with ambition. Without a floor plan, some of them book a showing just to find out, and leave in nine minutes.

That showing looks like activity. It isn't. It's you cleaning the house and leaving for an hour so someone can learn what a drawing would have told them. The floor plan trades those away for the buyers who were scrolling past because they couldn't tell how the house worked.

## Judging the Work Before You Hire

Ask for two or three complete recent listings from a photographer, not a highlight reel. A portfolio is the best frames from a hundred shoots. A full listing is what you're actually buying.

Look at the sequence: does it walk the house the way a person would move through it, or jump from the kitchen to a bedroom to the exterior and back? Look at consistency — are the whites the same room to room, are the verticals straight, do the windows show what's outside or blow out to white? In a valley where the view out the window is often the reason someone buys, blown-out windows are a real loss.

And look at the ordinary houses in their portfolio, not the architectural ones. Anyone can photograph a new build on Whitefish Lake. What matters is what they did with a well-kept 1994 ranch on a normal street — that's the assignment most of the time.

## The Question Worth Sitting With

Pull up your own listing on your phone, or a neighbor's if you aren't listed yet. Scroll it the way a stranger would — fast, unimpressed, with four other tabs open.

Does it hold you? Or does your eye slide off it?

That's the whole test, and you already know the answer.
