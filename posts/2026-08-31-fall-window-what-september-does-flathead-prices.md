If you've had your home on the market this summer and it hasn't sold, somebody has probably already told you the same thing: pull it, wait for spring, relist in April.

Here's the question worth asking before you take that advice: what do you actually believe happens between now and then?

Because the honest answer for most sellers isn't a strategy. It's a hope that a different season will fix a problem that isn't seasonal.

It's Monday. Numbers first, then the read.

## The Numbers: Labor Day Week 2026

**Kalispell**, for the period July 29 through August 29, 2026 (source: Montana Regional MLS data via IDEAL Real Estate's Kalispell market report, that period):

- New listings: 100, down 5.7% from the prior month
- Active inventory: 506 properties, down 1.9%
- Days on market, active listings: 114, up 9.3%
- Price changes: **162 listings adjusted their price, up 47.3%**
- Average list price, active inventory: $981,000

**Whitefish**, for July 30 through August 30, 2026 (same source, Whitefish report): 47 new listings, down 20.3%. Active inventory 298, down 4.8%. Days on market 152, up 10.6%. Average asking price north of $2.3 million.

**The wider region.** The most recent monthly figures for Flathead, Lincoln, and Lake counties remain July 2026: median sales price $612,500, down 2.8% year over year; 1,479 homes for sale, up 11.5% from July 2025; average days on market 104; sellers receiving 97.3% of asking price (source: Northwest Montana Association of REALTORS®, July 2026 market update, published August 17). The August figures won't publish until mid-September.

**Rates.** The Freddie Mac Primary Mortgage Market Survey put the 30-year fixed at 6.66% as of August 27, 2026 — up a single basis point from 6.65% the week before, with the 15-year at 5.98% (source: Freddie Mac PMMS, August 27, 2026). A year ago the 30-year averaged 6.56%. Rates are flat and have been flat all summer.

## What Fall Actually Does

Let me be straight about what I can and can't prove here. I don't have a clean multi-year study of Flathead September closings to hand you, and I'm not going to invent one. What I can tell you is the mechanism, and you can watch it happen in the numbers above.

Fall doesn't change what buyers will pay. It changes **what they're choosing between**.

New listings in Kalispell are down again, and down 20% in Whitefish. That decline accelerates through the fall — nobody puts a house on the market Thanksgiving week. Supply thins.

Demand thins too. But it thins differently: it doesn't shrink so much as it *concentrates*.

The June buyer was often browsing — vacationing, half-looking, curious what a place near the lake runs. The person still touring homes the second week of November is not browsing. They have a start date, a closing on the other end, a lease expiring, or a reason they can't wait until spring.

So the ratio moves. Fewer houses competing, and the shoppers who remain are disproportionately the serious ones. That's the fall window. It isn't a price rally. It's a change in who's in the room.

## The Number That Should Actually Worry You

One hundred sixty-two price changes in Kalispell in a single month, up 47%.

That is nearly a third of all active inventory adjusting its number in thirty days. And notice it's happening while inventory is *shrinking* — the market isn't correcting because of a supply flood. It's correcting because summer asking prices were ahead of the buyers.

If your home is sitting at or past that 114-day average, you're already in that cohort. You just haven't filed the paperwork.

And here's what "wait for spring" costs you: your listing sits accumulating days on market, or you withdraw and relist later carrying a stale history any decent buyer's agent will pull in about nine seconds. Meanwhile the spring you're waiting for is the season when *everyone* lists. Your competition in April is every seller who took the same advice you did, plus the natural spring inventory, all at once.

You'd be trading a thin-competition fall for a crowded spring, and paying carrying costs for seven months to do it.

## If You're Buying

The leverage is real, and it has an expiration date.

A seller at 114 days who has already cut once — or twice, given those 162 adjustments — is doing math at the kitchen table every night about carrying that house through a Montana winter. That's where concessions live: rate buydowns, closing credits, repair money that simply did not exist in June.

Rates aren't rescuing anyone. 6.66%, one basis point off the prior week, a tenth of a point *above* last August. If your plan is to wait for cheaper money, understand that cheaper money arrives for everyone simultaneously — and you'll be bidding against every other person who waited. The price you negotiate in a quiet October is permanent. The rate is the part you can refinance.

## The Bottom Line

September through November isn't the market's dead zone. It's the market's *filtered* zone — less competing inventory, fewer casual shoppers, and both sides finally motivated by something more concrete than a season.

What it will not do is rescue a price the summer already rejected. A home priced to what closed nearby in the last 60 days sells this fall. A home priced to June's optimism sits until it becomes the 163rd price change.

So the question worth sitting with this week: if you're selling, is "wait for spring" an actual plan — or is it just the least uncomfortable thing to do with a listing that hasn't sold? And if you're buying, which of those 114-day Kalispell listings would you look at differently knowing the seller is now staring at a winter of carrying costs?
