# Well and Septic in the Flathead Valley: The Due Diligence Most Buyers Skip

Here's a question most buyers never think to ask until it's too late: if the home you're about to buy sits outside city limits, do you actually know where your water comes from — and where it goes when you're done with it?

In the Flathead Valley, once you leave the city services of Kalispell, Whitefish, or Columbia Falls, the answer is almost always a private well and a septic system. And if you're moving from town — or relocating from out of state — there's a good chance you've never owned either one.

That's not a problem. Skipping the due diligence on them is.

I brief my buyers on wells and septic the same way I'd brief any mission: know the terrain before you commit. Here's the checklist.

## Start With the Well Log

Every drilled well in Montana should have a well log on file — a record created when the well was drilled that tells you the depth, the static water level, the flow rate at completion, and what the driller punched through to get there.

You can look these up yourself through the Montana Bureau of Mines and Geology's Ground Water Information Center (GWIC) database. It's free, it's public, and it takes about ten minutes.

What you're looking for: how deep is the well, when was it drilled, and what did it produce at the time? A well drilled in 1978 producing 8 gallons per minute isn't automatically a problem — but it's a data point you want before you're under contract, not after.

If there's no log on file, that's not an automatic walk-away either. But it moves testing from "recommended" to "non-negotiable."

## Test the Flow, Then Test the Water

The well log tells you what the well did the day it was drilled. It doesn't tell you what it does today. That's what a flow test is for.

A proper flow test runs the well for an extended period — not thirty seconds at the kitchen tap — and measures whether it sustains production. Wells can slow down over decades. Aquifers shift. You want to know the well keeps up with a family's real usage: laundry, showers, irrigation, all on the same Saturday.

Water quality is the second half. At minimum, test for coliform bacteria and nitrates. Depending on the property and the area, your inspector may recommend adding tests for things like arsenic or other naturally occurring minerals — some parts of Montana have them, and you won't taste them.

Budget-wise, think of flow and quality testing together as a few hundred dollars, give or take, depending on how many panels you run. Against the price of a home — and the cost of drilling a new well, which can run well into five figures — it's the cheapest insurance in the transaction.

## The Septic System: Pump It, Inspect It, Pull the Permit

Here's what most people get wrong about septic: they treat the inspection as optional because the seller says "it's never had a problem."

A septic system can be quietly failing for years before anyone notices a problem above ground. The only way to know is a real inspection — which means the tank gets pumped and a qualified inspector actually looks at the tank, the baffles, and the drainfield's condition. An inspection without pumping is a guess.

While that's being scheduled, pull the paperwork. Septic permits in this county run through the Flathead City-County Health Department. The permit record tells you when the system was installed, what it was designed for, and whether it was permitted at all.

That last part matters more than you'd think. A three-bedroom home with a septic system permitted for two bedrooms is a real conversation — about capacity, about future resale, and sometimes about price.

## Write the Contingency Like You Mean It

All of this only protects you if it's in the contract.

Your inspection contingency should specifically cover well and septic — with enough time to actually complete the work. Pumping and inspecting a septic tank, running a flow test, and getting water samples through a lab isn't a 48-hour turnaround, especially in the busy season when every inspector in the valley is booked.

Talk to your agent about the timeline before you write the offer, not after. And if the tests turn something up, the contingency is what gives you room to negotiate a repair, a credit, or — if it comes to it — a clean exit with your earnest money intact.

## The October Problem

Why does all this matter so much? Because the failure mode is predictable.

Buyer closes in July. Skips the flow test because the water ran fine during the showing. Skips the septic inspection because the seller's disclosure said no known issues. Then October arrives, the house is full, the laundry is running daily — and the well can't keep up, or the drainfield backs up after the first hard freeze.

Now it's not a negotiation. It's a repair bill. And the leverage you had under contract is gone.

Due diligence isn't about distrusting anyone. It's about knowing exactly what you're buying before your name is on it. That's how I run every transaction — no surprises, no assumptions, everything verified.

## Before You Write That Offer

So here's the question worth sitting with: if the home you're looking at is on a well and septic, can you say — with evidence, not assurances — that both systems will do their job for the next ten years?
