Every transaction has two versions of what happens next. There's the one everybody's hoping for — clean appraisal, clean inspection, financing clears on schedule, keys change hands on the date printed on the contract. And there's the other one, the one nobody wants to talk about at the kitchen table: the appraisal comes in short, the buyer's financing falls apart with ten days left, the inspector finds something behind a wall nobody knew was there.

Most people only plan for the first version. That's backwards.

## The Habit That Doesn't Come From Real Estate

Before any operation, there's a primary plan and there's a contingency — and the contingency doesn't get written in the middle of the crisis it's meant to solve. It gets written before anyone steps off, while everyone's calm and thinking clearly, precisely because that's the only time anyone thinks clearly. By the time something actually goes wrong, the plan already exists. Nobody's inventing a response under pressure. They're just executing the next page.

That habit doesn't turn off when the work changes. It shows up now in how a file gets prepared before a client ever asks a question, in how comps get run before they're needed rather than after, and in the fact that a contingency for the deal already exists before the deal needs one. It's not a personality quirk. It's a service standard — the same one that got applied under considerably higher stakes, just aimed at a closing table instead.

## What "Having a Plan" Actually Looks Like in a Transaction

Talk to enough agents and you'll hear a lot of confidence and not much specificity. Confidence isn't the same as a plan. A plan has to answer real questions, in advance, before anyone's emotional about the answer:

**If the appraisal comes in short** — is the buyer prepared to bring the difference in cash, is the seller open to a partial price adjustment, or does the contract get renegotiated? That conversation should happen before an appraiser ever sets foot on the property, not after the number lands and everyone's scrambling.

**If financing falls apart late** — what's the buyer's backup lender, how fast can they actually move, and at what point does the seller need to know the listing might need to go back on the market? Ten days from closing is not when you want to be asking these questions for the first time.

**If the inspection turns up something structural** — is there already a sense of what repairs the seller will negotiate versus walk away from, and does the buyer have a number in mind for what changes the deal versus what they can live with? A pre-decided range keeps a hard conversation from turning into a stalled one.

**If the buyer's own sale collapses** — was there ever a contingency built around it in the first place, and if not, what's the fallback timeline everyone agreed to going in?

None of these are exotic. Every experienced agent has seen versions of all four. The difference isn't whether the scenario gets anticipated — it's whether it got discussed on a calm Tuesday afternoon or discovered on a panicked Thursday night with a closing date bearing down.

## Why This Matters More Than It Sounds Like It Should

Here's the part that actually changes the client experience: when something does go sideways — and across enough transactions, something eventually does — the call that delivers the bad news is not the same call that leaves someone standing there with no idea what happens next. If the contingency was built ahead of time, that same phone call also delivers the next three steps. The client hears "here's what happened, and here's exactly what we do now" in the same breath.

Compare that to the alternative: the news lands, and now everyone's improvising a response to a problem that could have been anticipated a month earlier. That's where transactions actually fall apart — not usually because the underlying problem was unsolvable, but because nobody had a plan when it showed up, and improvised decisions under pressure are rarely anyone's best ones.

Panic, in a real estate transaction, is almost always a planning failure. It is not a personality failure, and it's not bad luck. It's a gap that existed before the stressful moment ever arrived, and it was closeable in advance.
