# Buying Land in the Flathead: What to Check Before the View

Here's a question worth asking before you make an offer on that parcel: if you couldn't see it — if all you had was the paperwork — would you still buy it?

Because land is the one purchase where the listing photos tell you almost nothing. A house shows you its problems eventually — a roof leaks, an inspector writes it all down. Twenty acres with a view of the Swan Range shows you exactly one thing: a view of the Swan Range. Everything that determines whether you can build on it is invisible, and most of it lives in a county file, a state database, or three feet underground.

I've watched buyers fall for a parcel in ninety seconds, then spend a year finding out what they bought. Here's the reconnaissance to run first.

## Access Comes Before the View

The first question is the least romantic one: can you legally and physically get to it?

Those are two different questions, and buyers routinely confuse them. Physical access means a driveable route exists. Legal access means you have a recorded, permanent right to use it — an easement that runs with the land and shows up in a title search.

"The neighbor's always let us drive through" is not legal access. That's a favor, and favors end when the neighbor sells.

So pull the title commitment early and read the easements. Find out who maintains the road and whether there's a written maintenance agreement — a shared gravel road with no agreement and six owners becomes its own small war every spring. Ask whether the county plows it. Most rural roads out here, they don't.

And if a parcel touches state trust land, public ownership next door is not the same as a right to cross it.

## Zoning: What You're Actually Allowed to Build

Not all of Flathead County is zoned, and that surprises people in both directions — some assume everything is regulated, others that nothing is.

Zoning outside city limits runs through the Flathead County Planning and Zoning Office, and you can pull a parcel's zoning district yourself on the county's interactive mapping site — search by address, owner, or assessor number and open the Parcel Information Report. Inside Kalispell, Whitefish, or Columbia Falls city limits, that city's planning office governs instead.

What you're looking for: the zoning district, minimum lot size, setbacks, and whether what you have in mind — a shop, a guest house, a short-term rental, an ag use, a home business — is permitted outright or needs a conditional use permit.

The county also has a Planner on Duty for short walk-in questions on weekdays — a free, underused resource, and one conversation there can save you an expensive assumption.

Check covenants, too. A subdivision's CCRs can be stricter than county zoning, and they're enforced by neighbors rather than a department.

## Water — And the Rule That Changed This Year

No municipal water means you're drilling a well, and Montana's rules changed recently enough that even people who bought land here five years ago are working off stale information.

The framework: small groundwater developments — 35 gallons per minute or less, up to 10 acre-feet per year — qualify for an exception to water-rights permitting and receive a Groundwater Certificate rather than a permit. But under the combined appropriation rule, two or more wells drawing from the same source and exceeding 10 acre-feet together need a permit regardless of flow rate. A 2024 district court decision in the Horse Creek Hills case tightened how that applies to phased subdivisions.

The newer change: under House Bill 681, effective January 1, 2026, you must file a Notice of Intent with DNRC before developing an exempt well, with the department reviewing within ten business days. Authorized notices expire after five years, with one possible five-year extension.

The framework is also actively contested in ongoing litigation, so verify current requirements with DNRC directly rather than relying on any article, including this one. I'm a real estate agent, not a water-rights attorney. If water is central to your plans, talk to one.

Practically: ask what neighboring wells produce and how deep they had to go. The Montana Bureau of Mines and Geology's GWIC database is free and public, and nearby well logs are the best preview of what you'll hit.

## Septic, Soils, and the Last Half Mile of Utilities

No sewer means a septic system, and not every piece of ground will take one.

Septic permitting in this county runs through the Flathead City-County Health Department, and approval depends on soil conditions, depth to groundwater, separation from wells and surface water, and system size. The test is a soil evaluation on the actual parcel — not a guess from the road.

Get it scheduled during due diligence, not after closing. A parcel that won't perc is one you can't build on the way you imagined — a price conversation at best.

Then price the utilities. Ask the electric provider what a service extension to your building site costs — power at the property line and power at the far corner of an eighty are very different numbers, sometimes tens of thousands apart. Ask the same about fiber if you work from home, and confirm a propane truck can reach the site in winter.

Finally, check FEMA floodplain mapping and the National Wetlands Inventory before planning a building envelope. Wetlands don't always look like wetlands in August. Look at wildfire exposure honestly too — defensible space, a second way out, and what insurance will cost. In this valley, insurability is part of buildability.

## Give the Due Diligence Window Room to Breathe

None of this fits in a ten-day inspection period.

Soil evaluations, title review, county conversations, utility quotes, and a survey take weeks, and in the busy season every inspector and excavator in the valley is booked out. Build the timeline into the offer, so your contingency covers the work instead of expiring in the middle of it.

That's how I brief every land buyer: know the terrain before you commit. For contract language, consult your attorney.

## Before You Fall in Love With the View

So here's the question to sit with: on the parcel you're considering, could you answer access, zoning, water, septic, and utilities with evidence — not with what the listing says?

If any one of those is a shrug, that's the conversation to have before you're under contract.
