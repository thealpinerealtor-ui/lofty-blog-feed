# Dividing the Equity: What the House Is Actually Worth in a Divorce

Most couples arguing about the house are arguing about the wrong number.

Not the wrong amount — the wrong *number entirely*. One of you is quoting an appraisal. The other is quoting what the neighbors got last spring. And the figure that actually lands on the settlement statement and gets divided between you is a third number neither of you has seen yet. That's usually the whole fight.

## Three numbers, and only one of them gets split

**Number one: appraised value.** An appraiser's job is to produce a defensible opinion of value on a specific date, based on closed comparable sales. It's backward-looking by design. In a divorce, it's often ordered because a court or an attorney needs a neutral figure on the record — which is exactly what it's good for.

**Number two: what the market will actually pay.** This is a forward-looking question, and buyers answer it, not a report. In the Flathead Valley right now, with a large share of active listings having already cut their asking price at least once, the gap between an appraisal and a real offer is wider than it was three years ago.

Neither number is wrong. They're answering different questions. The trouble starts when one spouse treats an appraisal as a sale price and the other treats an online estimate as an appraisal.

**Number three: net proceeds.** This is the number that gets split. It's what's left in the wire after the mortgage is paid off, the closing costs come out, the prorations settle, and any repairs the buyer negotiated are credited. It is always lower than number one and number two. Sometimes it's dramatically lower.

If your settlement conversation is happening in terms of numbers one or two, you're negotiating over money that doesn't exist yet.

## Where the money actually goes between contract and closing

Here's the walk from sale price down to what you'd divide. None of this is legal or tax advice — confirm your specific split with your attorney and your CPA — but this is the sequence, and knowing it early takes a lot of heat out of the room.

**The mortgage payoff isn't your last statement balance.** The payoff figure the title company orders includes per-diem interest through the actual closing date, plus any recording or demand fees the servicer charges. It runs a bit higher than the balance you're looking at online.

**Second mortgages, HELOCs, and liens come off next.** A home equity line drawn during the marriage is still a lien on the property. So is a contractor's lien, an unpaid tax lien, or a judgment attached to either spouse. Title work surfaces all of it — sometimes for the first time. Better to know in week one than week six.

**Brokerage compensation.** Real estate commissions are fully negotiable and are agreed to in writing before a home is listed. What matters for planning is simply that whatever is agreed comes off the top, and both spouses should see that figure in writing before anyone signs a listing agreement — not after.

**Closing costs and title.** Owner's title insurance, the closing or escrow fee, recording fees, and the well and septic testing a Montana buyer will typically require. One piece of good news: Montana has no real estate transfer tax. A Realty Transfer Certificate is filed with the county at closing, but there's no percentage-of-price tax the way there is in many states.

**Property tax proration.** Montana property taxes are billed in two installments — end of November and end of May. Depending on when you close, you may owe the buyer a credit for taxes accrued but not yet paid. On a higher-value Flathead property, that's a real line item.

**Repair credits and buyer concessions.** In this market, buyers are asking. Inspection credits and rate buydowns come out of the seller's proceeds. Budget a number for it in advance, because that negotiation lands right when both of you are worn out.

**Capital gains — ask your CPA, early.** The primary-residence exclusion, and how it applies when the marriage ends mid-year, is a question for a tax professional. Ask before you agree to a split, not after. The timing of the sale relative to the divorce can matter.

## Why a net sheet early ends arguments

A seller's net sheet is a one-page estimate that runs sale price down through every line above to a projected number in hand. It takes very little time to produce, it can be run at three different prices, and it costs nothing.

Here's why it matters more in a divorce than in any other sale: it moves the conversation from *positions* to *arithmetic*. When two people are arguing about whether the house is worth $625,000 or $680,000, they're arguing about fairness and the last several years of their lives. When they're both looking at the same sheet showing what each price actually nets, the disagreement usually shrinks to something specific and solvable — and the two prices often differ by less than the argument is costing.

I'll run that sheet for both spouses at the same time, with the same assumptions, and send it to both of you and to both attorneys together. Same document, same moment, no side conversations. In this work, neutrality isn't a posture — it's a set of procedures, and that's one of them.

## A question worth sitting with

If you and your spouse both wrote down what you think the house is worth, and then both wrote down what you think you'd each walk away with — how far apart would those second numbers be?

That gap is the one worth closing first. It's also the cheapest one to close, because it takes a spreadsheet rather than a fight.
