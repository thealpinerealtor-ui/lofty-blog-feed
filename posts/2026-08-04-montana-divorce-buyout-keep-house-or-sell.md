# Keep the House or Sell It? The Montana Divorce Buyout, Explained

If you're going through a divorce in the Flathead Valley and one of you wants to keep the house, you've probably already had some version of this conversation: "You keep the house, I'll take my share, and we move on."

It sounds simple. It's usually the most complicated financial move in the entire divorce.

Here's what most people get wrong about buyouts: they think the hard part is agreeing to it. The hard part is executing it. A buyout is really three separate problems — agreeing on what the house is worth, getting one name off the mortgage, and finding the money to pay out the other person's share. Miss any one of the three, and the deal that sounded so clean in the kitchen falls apart months later, usually at the worst possible time.

Let's walk through each one, so you can tell early whether a buyout actually pencils for your situation — or whether selling is the kinder path for both of you.

## Problem one: what is the house actually worth?

Everything downstream depends on this number, and it's the first place buyouts go sideways.

The spouse keeping the house has every incentive to see a lower value. The spouse being bought out has every incentive to see a higher one. Zillow doesn't settle it — automated estimates in a market like ours, with everything from in-town Kalispell homes to acreage properties with wells and shops, can miss by a wide margin.

What typically works: a professional appraisal, or sometimes two, with a method for reconciling them agreed on in advance. Some couples use a broker price opinion as a starting point and an appraisal to finalize. The specific mechanism matters less than agreeing on the mechanism *before* you see the number. Once a number is on the table, neutrality gets a lot harder.

One more wrinkle worth knowing: in a divorce settlement, the buyout price is negotiable in a way the open market isn't. Some couples discount slightly from appraised value to reflect the selling costs a real sale would have carried. That's a conversation for your attorneys — but know that it's a lever.

## Problem two: the refinance nobody budgets for

Here's the part that quietly kills more buyouts than the valuation fight ever does.

Agreeing that one spouse keeps the house does not take the other spouse off the mortgage. The lender isn't a party to your divorce decree. If both names are on the note, both of you remain fully liable — even after the decree says the house belongs to one of you. The only clean way off the note is a refinance in the keeping spouse's name alone (or, occasionally, a loan assumption if the loan allows it — ask the servicer, and don't assume).

That means the spouse keeping the house has to qualify for the mortgage *by themselves* — one income, and often a larger loan than the original, because the refinance frequently has to pull out cash to fund the equity payment. Qualifying solo at today's rates, for a bigger loan, on one income, right after a divorce has split the household finances in half: that's the test. A meaningful number of people don't pass it.

If you're the spouse being bought out, this matters just as much. Never let the decree say "spouse keeps the house and will refinance eventually." Insist on a deadline — commonly 90 to 180 days — with an automatic sale provision if the refinance doesn't happen. Your attorney will have language for this. Without it, your name can stay on that note for years, dragging on your credit and your ability to buy your own next home.

## Problem three: where does the equity payment come from?

Flathead Valley homeowners who bought before the run-up are often sitting on substantial equity. That's good news and bad news in a buyout: there's real money to divide, and real money that has to come from somewhere.

The usual sources are cash-out proceeds from the refinance, offsetting assets — one spouse takes the house, the other takes retirement accounts or other property of comparable value — or some combination. Offsetting sounds tidy, but a dollar of home equity and a dollar in a retirement account aren't the same dollar once taxes and access rules enter the picture. This is exactly where "consult your attorney and your CPA" isn't a disclaimer — it's the actual advice.

## When the buyout doesn't pencil — and what that means

Run the three tests honestly. Can you agree on a value? Can the keeping spouse qualify alone? Is there a real source for the equity payment? If any answer is no, the buyout doesn't pencil — and it's far better to know that now than after months of fighting for a house you can't actually afford to keep.

And here's the reframe worth sitting with: selling isn't losing. A sale converts the fight into a number, splits it cleanly, gets both names off the debt on day one, and lets two people start their next chapter with cash instead of entanglement. I've watched buyouts succeed and I've watched them strain budgets for years. Sometimes the house one spouse "won" becomes the anchor that keeps the divorce from ever really ending.

There's no judgment in either answer. The goal is a decision made on numbers, early, instead of emotion, late.

## A question to sit with

If you're facing this decision, ask yourself: am I trying to keep the house, or am I trying to keep what the house represents? Those are different things, and only one of them can be refinanced.
