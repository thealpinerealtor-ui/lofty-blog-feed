# The Buyout Loan: Why Refinancing to Keep the House Is Harder Than It Sounds

If you've said the words "I'll just refinance and keep the house" out loud to your attorney, you're not alone. It's the first plan almost every spouse floats in a Flathead Valley divorce. It's also, more often than any other plan, the one that quietly falls apart three months later — not because anyone changed their mind, but because nobody walked through what a buyout refinance actually requires before it got written into the settlement.

This isn't legal or tax advice, and it isn't a case for keeping the house or selling it. Your attorney and your lender own those numbers. What follows is the sequence — the steps a buyout refinance has to clear, in order, and where each one tends to snag.

## You're qualifying alone now, on one income

The mortgage on the house was likely approved on two incomes and two sets of debts. A buyout refinance has to qualify on one of each. That sounds obvious stated plainly, but it's the step most people haven't priced out before it's in the settlement agreement — a debt-to-income ratio that worked fine for a household of two can miss the mark for a household of one, especially once spousal support, if any, is added to the liability side of that math rather than the income side, or not counted as income at all depending on how long it's guaranteed to continue. The lender runs this number. It doesn't care what the decree says should happen — only whether the person keeping the house can actually carry the loan alone.

## The appraisal doesn't know what number is in the settlement

Somewhere in the settlement conversation, a home value got agreed to — sometimes from a comparative market analysis, sometimes from a number one spouse remembered from three years ago, sometimes from what felt fair. The refinance doesn't use that number. It uses a fresh appraisal, ordered fresh, valuing the house as of today.

If that appraisal comes in lower than the figure written into the settlement, the equity the other spouse is owed doesn't shrink to match — but the amount available to refinance against does. That gap has to be closed with cash, a renegotiated split, or a different plan entirely, and it's a far better conversation to have before the settlement is signed than after the appraisal report lands.

## The spouse being bought out is still on the loan — and the liability — until closing actually happens

This is the one that catches people off guard. A settlement agreement can say the house belongs to one spouse and the other is being bought out. That agreement does not remove either name from the existing mortgage. Only the new loan closing does that. Until the refinance actually funds, both spouses remain contractually liable for the original mortgage, whatever the decree says about who's responsible for what. A late payment during that window shows up on both credit reports. "The divorce is final" and "the refinance closed" are two different dates, and the gap between them is where risk sits for the spouse who's supposed to be out.

## Timing the closing against a deadline that doesn't move for a lender

Settlement agreements often include a date: refinance and remove the other spouse's name by such-and-such, or the house gets listed instead. Lenders don't work around court deadlines — they work through underwriting timelines, appraisal scheduling, and title work, the same as any other refinance. Building a 30-day cushion between the loan's realistic closing date and the settlement's deadline isn't overcautious. It's the difference between a clean transition and a scramble that lands back in front of the judge.

## When the refinancing spouse can't qualify for the full amount

Sometimes the numbers simply don't work — income doesn't support the loan amount needed to pay out the other spouse's share, even with the house's full equity behind it. This isn't a failure, and it isn't a dead end. It's information, and it usually means one of a few paths: a smaller buyout paired with a payment plan for the remainder, a co-signer, a longer runway before refinancing is attempted again, or — for some couples — selling the house and splitting the proceeds turns out to be the cleaner outcome for both people once the refinance math is actually run rather than assumed.

## A question worth asking before it's in writing

If your settlement includes a buyout refinance, has anyone actually run the numbers — income, appraisal, timeline — or is the plan resting on what felt fair in a conversation?

That's not a question your attorney can answer alone, and it's not one I can answer instead of a lender. But it's the question worth asking before the number goes into the agreement, not after.
