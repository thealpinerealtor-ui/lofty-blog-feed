# How Long Montana Probate Actually Takes Before a House Can List

If someone just passed away and you're already being asked when the house will "go on the market," here's the question worth sitting with first: who actually has the legal authority to sign the listing agreement right now? For most families, the honest answer is nobody — not yet.

That catches people off guard. The assumption is usually that a home can list within a week or two of a funeral, as soon as the family agrees it's time. In practice, Montana probate has to hand someone formal authority to act on the estate's behalf before a listing agreement is worth the paper it's signed on. Here's what that timeline realistically looks like, and what tends to add weeks to it.

## Why "right after the funeral" isn't how this works

A house doesn't become sellable the moment someone is gone — it becomes sellable once a personal representative (Montana's term for what some states call an executor or administrator) is formally appointed by the court and holds documented authority over the estate's assets, including the real property. Until that happens, no one can bind the estate to a listing agreement, a purchase and sale contract, or a closing. That's true even when every heir agrees on the plan and even when the family already has a buyer lined up informally.

This isn't bureaucracy for its own sake. It exists so that whoever signs on the estate's behalf can actually convey clear title — which is what both the buyer's lender and the title company will insist on before closing regardless of how eager everyone is to move.

## Informal vs. formal probate — the fork that decides your timeline

Montana offers two main tracks, and which one applies has the biggest effect on how long this takes.

**Informal probate** is the faster, more common path when there's a valid will (or a clear path under intestacy law), no real dispute among heirs, and nothing unusual about the estate. A district court clerk — not a judge — can appoint the personal representative, often within a few weeks of filing. Once appointed, the PR receives Letters Testamentary (with a will) or Letters of Administration (without one), which is the actual document a listing agreement and a buyer's title company will want to see.

**Formal probate** gets involved when there's a will contest, uncertainty about who the rightful heirs are, disagreement over who should serve as personal representative, or other complications. This track requires a judge, often a hearing, and routinely adds a month or more before anyone is appointed — sometimes considerably longer if the dispute is contested.

Most straightforward family situations in the Flathead qualify for informal probate. But "most" isn't "all," and it's worth finding that out early rather than assuming.

## What "authority to sign" actually looks like on paper

Letters Testamentary or Letters of Administration are what a listing brokerage and, later, a title company will ask to see before treating a sale as real. Until those are issued, a family can still prepare the house, gather documents, get it appraised, or have informal conversations with an agent about pricing and timing — but the listing agreement itself isn't enforceable, and a buyer's lender won't move toward closing on a contract that no one had the authority to sign.

Practically, that means the useful early work — decluttering, minor repairs, pulling together the deed and tax records, deciding on a target price range — doesn't have to wait on the court. The signature does.

## The creditor-claim window, and what else adds weeks

Once a personal representative is appointed, Montana law requires a notice-to-creditors period, giving the estate's creditors a window to file claims before certain distributions are considered final. That window runs in parallel with marketing the home, so it rarely blocks a listing outright — but it's one more piece of the overall timeline families don't always know to expect.

Beyond that, a few things reliably add real weeks to a probate sale:

A missing or unclear will forces the informal path toward formal probate, since the court needs to sort out who the proper heirs are before anyone gets appointed. Out-of-state heirs can slow things down when documents require notarized signatures or in-person steps that take longer to coordinate remotely. And disagreement among heirs — even mild, well-intentioned disagreement about price, timing, or whether to sell at all — is probably the single most common delay, because it can push an otherwise informal case toward formal proceedings.

Knowing which of these applies to your situation early is what keeps a family from guessing for weeks before finding out the real timeline.

## Where this leaves a family

None of this is a reason to wait to start preparing — it's a reason to find out, early, which track your situation is actually on. An estate attorney confirms the specifics for your probate; this is a starting orientation, not a determination of your case.

So here's the question worth asking before anything else: has a personal representative actually been appointed yet, or is that step still in progress? If you're not sure, that's a completely normal place to be — and a good first conversation to have before you start planning around a listing date.
