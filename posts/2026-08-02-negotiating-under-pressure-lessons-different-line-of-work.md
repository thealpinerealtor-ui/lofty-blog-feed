# Negotiating Under Pressure: Lessons From a Different Line of Work

Here's a question worth sitting with for a second: when the stakes go up in a negotiation, do you get louder — or quieter?

Most people assume the strong negotiator is the aggressive one. The table-pounder. The person who "wins" the conversation. Before real estate, I spent years in a line of work — the Army, and later protective services for the US government — where I watched genuinely high-stakes negotiations happen, sometimes with real consequences attached. And here's what surprised me most: the best people in the room almost never raised their voices.

They didn't need to. They'd already won before the conversation started. What follows are the three habits I watched save missions overseas — and how each one shows up, almost unchanged, in a Flathead Valley real estate deal.

## Preparation is the whole game

In mission planning, there's a saying: the plan is decided before anyone steps out the door. By the time things get tense, you're not thinking — you're executing decisions you already made when you were calm.

Real estate negotiation works exactly the same way, and most people get this backwards. They wait until the counteroffer lands, feel the spike of emotion, and then try to decide what they're willing to do. That's negotiating from the worst possible position: reactive, emotional, and on someone else's timeline.

When I sit down with a buyer before we write an offer on a Kalispell home, we make the hard decisions in advance. What's our true walk-away number — not the number we hope for, the number where we genuinely stand up from the table? What are we willing to concede on inspection, and what's non-negotiable? If they counter at full price with a short close, what's our answer?

None of those questions are fun. But answering them on a Tuesday afternoon over coffee is a completely different experience than answering them at 9 p.m. with a deadline expiring and a lender waiting. Sellers get the same briefing before we list: here's the likely range of offers, here's how we respond to each scenario, here's what we do if we get multiples — and here's what we do if we get silence.

When the pressure arrives, we're not deciding anymore. We're executing.

## Information discipline: say less, learn more

The second habit took me longer to appreciate. In protective work, information discipline isn't optional — what you reveal, when, and to whom is managed deliberately, because information is leverage and leverage protects people.

In real estate, almost nobody manages information deliberately. Buyers gush to listing agents about how much they love the house. Sellers let it slip that they've already bought in Bigfork and need to close fast. Every one of those disclosures moves money — real money — from one side of the table to the other.

Think about it from the other side. If you're a seller and you know the buyers are stretched to their maximum and emotionally attached, how do you respond to their inspection requests? Now reverse it: if you're a buyer and you know the sellers close on their next house in three weeks, what does your offer look like?

Discipline here isn't dishonesty — you never misrepresent anything, and Montana disclosure obligations are what they are. It's simply this: you don't volunteer leverage. My clients and I decide together, in advance, what we share and what stays inside the tent. And just as important, we listen. The other side is usually leaking information constantly. The quiet person in the negotiation is usually the one collecting it.

## Calm is a decision, not a personality trait

People sometimes assume calm under pressure is something you're born with. It isn't. It's trained. Every high-stakes profession — military, medicine, aviation — trains people to slow down precisely when instinct screams to speed up, because rushed decisions under stress are reliably bad decisions.

Real estate transactions manufacture pressure on purpose. Offer deadlines. Escalation clauses. "Another buyer is seeing it tonight." Inspection response windows. Some of that pressure is real, and some of it is theater — and the single most valuable thing I do for clients is help them tell the difference.

Here's a scenario I see constantly in the Whitefish market. A well-priced home hits on Thursday, showings stack up through the weekend, and by Sunday night my buyers are being told to submit their "highest and best" by noon Monday. The instinct is panic: waive everything, stretch the number, win at any cost. Sometimes an aggressive offer is exactly right. But that decision should come from the plan we built when we were calm — our real ceiling, our real risk tolerance — not from a wave of adrenaline at 11 p.m.

The counterintuitive truth is that the calm party usually takes the better deal, in negotiations of every kind. Not because calm is polite, but because calm people see clearly, and clear sight is worth money.

## What this means if you're buying or selling here

The Flathead Valley market has real stakes. For most families in Kalispell, Whitefish, or Columbia Falls, the home is the largest asset they'll ever negotiate over — and in complicated situations, a divorce sale or an estate settlement, the pressure is emotional as well as financial. That's exactly when preparation, information discipline, and calm stop being philosophy and start being the difference between a good outcome and a costly one.

You don't need a military background to negotiate well. You need a plan made in advance, discipline about what you reveal, and someone in your corner who doesn't flinch when the deadline lands.

So here's the question worth asking yourself: if the pressure moment in your next real estate deal arrived tomorrow — the counteroffer, the multiple-offer deadline, the inspection surprise — would you be deciding in the moment, or executing a plan you already made?
