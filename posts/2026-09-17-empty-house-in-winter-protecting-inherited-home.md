# The Empty House in Winter: Protecting an Inherited Home Until It Sells

Probate has its own calendar, and it rarely lines up with the one on the wall.

By the time an estate clears the paperwork, secures the personal representative's authority, and gets a house ready to list, it's not unusual for a Flathead property to sit empty through its first winter with nobody living in it. Nobody plans for that. It just happens — the legal side takes the time it takes, and the house sits there in the meantime, unattended, while temperatures do what they do in this valley from November through March.

An empty house doesn't fail all at once. It fails quietly, in ways nobody notices until spring, when the family walks in for a showing and finds out what a Flathead winter did to a place with no one watching it.

If you're a personal representative or an heir looking at a vacant house and a first freeze on the calendar, here's what actually needs handling — the practical care of an asset the family is holding on someone else's behalf, not the legal side, which is your attorney's to walk you through.

## Call the insurer before you call anyone else

This is the one people skip, and it's the one that costs the most when they do.

A standard homeowners policy is written for an occupied house. Most carriers have a vacancy clause that limits or excludes coverage — water damage in particular — once a home sits empty past a stated number of consecutive days, often 30 to 60 depending on the policy. If a pipe freezes and bursts in an unoccupied house and the insurer can show the vacancy exclusion applied, the estate can be looking at a claim that pays little or nothing on exactly the kind of loss an empty winter house is most exposed to.

Tell the carrier the home is vacant, in writing, the day it becomes vacant. Ask directly whether the existing policy still covers it, whether a vacant-home endorsement or a separate vacant-property policy is needed, and what conditions that coverage requires — a working heat source above a minimum temperature is a common one. This is a conversation for the estate's attorney and the insurer, not a guess on my end, but it needs to happen before the first hard freeze, not after a claim.

## Heat, water, and the pipes in between

Once coverage is sorted, the mechanical decision is heat left on versus the system drained. Both work; picking neither is what causes damage.

Left occupied-style, most guidance is to keep the thermostat no lower than the mid-50s, which keeps interior wall cavities above freezing without running a full heating bill on an empty house. The alternative is a full drain-down: water shut off at the main, lines blown out or drained, and antifreeze in traps and toilets — done properly, usually by a plumber, so nothing gets missed. A house that's heated but has water shut off and lines drained anyway is the safest combination, since a frozen pipe with no water behind it does far less damage than one that's pressurized.

Either way, pay attention to the pipes on exterior walls and in unheated spaces — garages, crawl spaces, an addition with thinner insulation — since those freeze first regardless of what the thermostat says. And don't forget the water heater; draining it or leaving it running is its own decision, not an afterthought to the rest of the system.

## Snow, ice, and a house that doesn't look empty

An inherited home still needs a driveway a plow can reach and a roofline that isn't building an ice dam over the gutters. Arrange snow removal on a standing schedule, not a call-when-someone-remembers basis — both so the house stays passable for showings once it's ready to list, and because an unplowed drive and an unshoveled walk are the fastest visual signal to a passerby that nobody's home.

Ice damming is worth a specific mention: heat escaping through an under-insulated attic melts snow on the roof, it refreezes at the colder eave, and water backs up under the shingles into the house. It's a slow, expensive kind of damage that shows up as a stain on a ceiling months later. A quick check of attic insulation and gutter clearing before the snow starts is cheap compared to what a winter of damming can do.

## A set of eyes, on a schedule

Mail piling up in a box, a light that's never on, snow that never gets tracked — these are the details that tell the wrong kind of visitor a house is unattended. Arrange for someone — a neighbor, a property-watch service, or a local contact — to physically check the house on a regular schedule through the winter: confirm heat is holding, look for water, collect mail, and make sure the place reads as looked-after even when it isn't lived in.

## The part worth saying out loud

None of this is legal work. It's the maintenance an estate has to fund and coordinate to protect an asset that isn't anyone's home anymore but still belongs to the family until it sells. Skipping it doesn't save money — it just moves the cost from a manageable winter bill to a spring repair, right when the house needs to show well.

So the question worth asking before the first hard freeze: does this house have a plan for the next four months, or does it just have an address?
