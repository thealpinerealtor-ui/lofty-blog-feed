# Pricing Into a Slow Market Without Getting Chased Down

There's a specific kind of misery in a slow market, and most sellers walk into it the same way.

You list high — you can always come down. Two weeks pass with three showings and no offers. You cut ten thousand. Nothing. A month later, fifteen more. By October you're forty thousand below where you started at 140 days on market, and the offers coming in are below your original number minus every cut you've made.

You didn't price the house. You chased the market down a hill.

## What the Valley Is Actually Doing

For the period July 23 through August 23, 2026, Kalispell showed 510 active properties and **157 price changes** — adjustments up 30.8% from the prior month, on inventory averaging 113 days on market (source: Montana Regional MLS data via IDEAL Real Estate's Kalispell market report, August 2026 period).

Roughly one in three active listings has already reduced.

Here's the part that matters more than the headline: across northwest Montana in July, sellers still received an average of **97.3% of asking price**, and closed sales were up 14.9% year over year (source: Northwest Montana Association of REALTORS®, July 2026 market update).

Read those together and they say something precise. Homes are selling, and sellers who close aren't getting gutted at the table. The haircut in this market lands on the *listing* price — before the sign ever goes up — not on the *right* price.

The money isn't lost in negotiation. It's lost in the first thirty days, at a number that was never real.

## The First Two Weeks Are the Only Honest Feedback You Get

When a home hits the market it gets a burst of attention that never comes back. Every buyer in your price band sees it that week. Every saved search fires. That audience is finite, and it shows up once.

If your number is wrong, they don't argue with you about it — they just don't come. And that's the trap: no showings *feels* like no information, so sellers wait. It's actually the clearest information you'll ever get.

Three mechanical questions at day 14:

- **How many showings?** Under five in two weeks in this market is a price signal, not a marketing problem.
- **Any second visits?** Traffic with no repeats usually means your house is the one buyers look at to feel good about something else.
- **What went under contract near you since you listed, and at what number?**

Sellers who reprice at day 14 usually reprice once. Sellers who wait until day 60 reprice three times.

## What an Aspirational Number Actually Costs

The pitch for pricing high is that it's free. It isn't. It costs you in three places.

**Days on market.** At 113 days average, you don't have much runway before a listing reads as stale. Buyers watch that number, and a house at 90-plus days invites a lower opening offer than the same house at 20 — usually lower than the reduction you were avoiding.

**Position in the search.** A $749,000 home listed at $799,000 stops competing with the $749,000 homes and starts losing to the $799,000 ones. Priced right, it's the best house in its bracket. Overpriced, it's the cautionary tale one bracket up.

**Your negotiating posture.** Every reduction is a public signal. Cut three times and buyers stop asking what the house is worth and start asking how much more you'll take.

The seasonal clock matters too. New listings in Kalispell were down 13.1% last month and inventory thins through fall — a real tailwind for a correctly priced home in September. But a house still chasing a spring number in November enters the slowest stretch of the year with the worst story on the sheet.

## A Reset vs. a Follow

Not all reductions are the same, and this is where sellers lose money quietly.

**A follow** is a small cut to stay just under the last comparable sale. Five thousand off, then five more. Each one keeps you in front of the same buyers who already passed, which is why nothing changes.

**A reset** is one deliberate move that puts your home in front of a *different* pool of buyers — under a search threshold, below the listing you keep losing to, at a number that makes your house the obvious value in its bracket. It generates new showings because it reaches people who never saw the listing.

Make it big enough to cross a real threshold: $625,000 to $599,000 reaches an entirely different search than $625,000 to $619,000. Make it once, and time it to a weekend.

One decisive reset almost always nets more than three apologetic ones.

## Setting a Number You Can Hold

The number you want is the one you won't be arguing with yourself about in six weeks. That's an exercise, not a feeling.

Start from what's actually **closed** within about a mile in the last 60 to 90 days — not what's listed, and not what your neighbor turned down in 2023. List prices are opinions. Closings are evidence. Then adjust honestly for what buyers pay for here: usable acreage, view and exposure, garage and shop space, well and septic condition, and how far you sit from a paved road in February.

Run your net sheet **before** you pick the price, not after you get an offer. If the number that works for your life is above what the comps support, that's worth knowing in August — while timing and prep are still options — rather than in November with three cuts behind you.

Then decide your reset in advance: *if we're under X showings with no second visits at day 14, we move to Y, once.* Making that call calmly on the front end is a completely different decision than making it at day 45 when the phone has stopped ringing.

Set a real number. Get honest feedback fast. If you have to move, move once and move decisively.

So the question worth sitting with this weekend: do you know what your street has actually closed at in the last 60 days — or is your price built on what you hoped it was worth in the spring?
