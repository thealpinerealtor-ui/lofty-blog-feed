# When the Right Advice Is "Don't"

When was the last time someone in a commission-based job told you not to do the thing that pays them?

It doesn't come up much, because it isn't supposed to. The incentive in this business runs one direction: a transaction closes, or it doesn't, and only one of those outcomes puts money in an agent's pocket. So the harder conversations this month — the ones about planning a sale around a divorce timeline, sequencing an estate through probate, reading an offer past the headline number — have all assumed the client and the agent want the same thing: to get to closing, cleanly. This one is about what happens when they don't.

## The Job Nobody Puts on the Business Card

Most of real estate is exactly what it looks like — pricing, marketing, negotiating, managing a timeline. But underneath that is a smaller, less comfortable job: telling a client that the deal sitting in front of them, the one they've already started picturing themselves living in or hoping to fund with, isn't the one to do. Not because the paperwork is wrong. Because the fit is.

That instinct isn't new to real estate for me. Years spent making calls where the cost of being wrong was measured in more than money teaches you a specific discipline: separate what you want to be true from what the situation is actually telling you, say the uncomfortable thing early, and let the decision-maker decide with full information rather than a comfortable version of it. Applied here, that means an agent's job isn't just to get a client to the table. It's to tell them, plainly, when the table isn't worth sitting at.

## Three Situations, Told Without Names

None of what follows is one specific client — these are composites, built from patterns that show up often enough to be worth writing down.

**The house that checks every box except one.** A buyer finds a place with the right bedroom count, the right school district, the right price, and a driveway that turns into a sheet of ice every winter because of how the lot sits relative to the sun. On a July showing, nobody notices. The box-checking is satisfying enough that the one real problem gets waved off as something to "figure out later." Later, for a lot of buyers, means a house they resent by February. Pointing that out before the offer goes in doesn't kill every deal like it — some buyers hear it and go forward anyway, eyes open, which is a fine outcome. But they heard it first.

**The offer that should be countered, not accepted.** A seller gets a number that's close enough to asking that the temptation is to just take it and be done — especially after a slower stretch on the market. Sometimes that instinct is right. Sometimes the offer is soft in a way the headline price hides: a long due-diligence period, financing that hasn't been verified past a form letter, a closing date that doesn't actually work for the seller's own next move. Accepting fast isn't the same as accepting well, and a seller who's tired of waiting is exactly the seller who needs someone to slow the moment down for thirty seconds before signing.

**The purchase that pencils out and shouldn't be stretched for.** A buyer can technically qualify for a number that leaves almost nothing left over once the mortgage, taxes, insurance, and the realities of an older home are all sitting on the same monthly statement. The math works on paper. It's the kind of math that works right up until a furnace fails in January or a job gets shakier than expected. Telling a buyer that qualifying for a number isn't the same as being able to live inside it comfortably is not the advice that gets an offer written today. It's usually the advice that keeps someone in their house five years from now.

## What It Actually Costs to Say No

Arguing against a transaction is not free for the person doing it. It costs a commission that might not come back around, at least not from that specific deal. It sometimes costs a client relationship if the advice lands wrong or the client wasn't ready to hear it. And it never comes with a thank-you in the moment — nobody feels grateful for being told to wait, or to pass, or to counter instead of just taking the win.

What it buys instead is longer and quieter: a client who, three years later, is still glad they didn't buy that house, or that they held out for a real offer instead of the first one. Those are the clients who refer people. Not because the transaction was exciting, but because someone was willing to be the person in the room whose job wasn't just to get the deal done.

## What This Actually Sounds Like

It's rarely dramatic. It sounds like "before you write this offer, let's talk about what the driveway does every winter." It sounds like "I'd counter this, and here's specifically why." It sounds like "you qualify for this, and I want to make sure you also want to live inside it." None of it is a war story. It's a standard, applied the same way whether the deal is easy or hard: say the true thing before the offer is signed, not after.

If you're in the middle of a decision right now and something about it doesn't sit right — even if you can't quite name what — that instinct is worth a conversation before it's worth an offer.
