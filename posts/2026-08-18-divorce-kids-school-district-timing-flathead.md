# Divorcing With Kids in the Flathead: Timing the House Around the School Year

School starts in the Flathead Valley in about a week. If you're a divorcing parent, you already know that — because the school calendar has quietly become one more thing attached to the house question.

Here's what most people in your situation are actually asking, even if they haven't said it out loud: *Can the kids stay in their school? And what does that mean for what we do with the house?*

Those are two different questions. Most couples treat them as one, and that's where the avoidable mistakes happen.

## The school district question comes first — not the house question

Keeping your kids in their current school doesn't automatically require keeping the current house. It requires an address inside the district boundary — or, in some cases, an approved out-of-district enrollment. Kalispell, Whitefish, Columbia Falls, Bigfork, Somers-Lakeside — each district draws its own lines, and in parts of the valley those lines run closer to your street than you'd guess.

So before anyone declares "we can't sell, the kids need their school," it's worth asking a narrower question: what would it take for at least one parent to live inside the boundary? Sometimes that's the current house. Sometimes it's a rental eight blocks away. Sometimes it's a smaller purchase that one income can actually carry.

The families who navigate this well figure out the school answer first, then let the house decision follow from it — not the other way around.

## Why the calendar matters more in a divorce sale

In an ordinary sale, listing timing is a pricing conversation. In a divorce sale with kids, it's also a stability conversation. A move over winter break lands differently than a move in the third week of September. A closing that forces a mid-semester school change is the kind of detail a Montana parenting plan discussion can end up caring about.

That's why some divorcing families in the valley list in the fall on purpose. It sounds backwards — everyone hears that spring is the season — but a fall listing can put closing and moving inside the semester break, give both parents the school year to settle their next housing, and let the family start the following school year with everything already decided. Fall buyers in Kalispell and Whitefish also tend to be fewer but more serious, which matters when you'd rather have one clean transaction than a parade of showings through a house where kids are doing homework.

I'm not telling you fall is the answer. I'm telling you the listing date is a decision, not a default — and in a divorce, it should be made looking at the school calendar and the custody schedule, not just the market chart.

## Custody schedules and showings: plan it like logistics, because it is

A house on the market gets shown, photographed, inspected, and appraised. With a custody schedule in play, every one of those events lands on somebody's parenting time.

The couples who keep this low-conflict handle it the way I'd handle any operation: agree on the rules before the first showing, in writing. Which days are available for showings. How much notice each parent gets. Who handles the house on their days. What the kids are told, and by whom. Where the pets go.

None of that is complicated. All of it prevents the 8 p.m. phone call that turns a scheduling hiccup into a court filing. As a neutral third party, part of my job is being the one who communicates with both spouses on the same protocol — same information, same time, no side channels — so the house doesn't become another front.

## The one conversation to have before anything goes on the market

Before a sign goes in the yard — before you even pick an agent — talk to your attorney about how the sale interacts with your case. A few specifics worth raising: whether the timing of listing and closing fits the parenting plan you're negotiating, how proceeds will be held and divided at closing, and whether any temporary orders in your case affect listing, showing, or accepting an offer.

I'm not an attorney, and nothing here is legal advice — Montana parenting plans and property division are exactly the things to run through your lawyer. But I can tell you from the real estate side: the sales that go smoothly are the ones where the legal timeline and the listing timeline were lined up on purpose, early. The painful ones are where the house got ahead of the case.

## What this looks like when it's done right

A clear phase plan: school decision, legal green light, prep, list, close — each with a date. Both spouses briefed the same way at the same time. Showings scheduled around the custody calendar instead of against it. A closing date chosen with the school calendar in view. No surprises, because surprises are the enemy.

Divorce is hard enough without the house becoming the battlefield. Handled with discipline, it's just a transaction — one with a start date, an end date, and two people who can both live with the result.
