# The Sibling Buyout: How Heirs Actually Price One Person Keeping the House

Three siblings inherit their parents' house in the Flathead Valley. Two are ready to sell. One wants to stay — maybe they've been the one checking on the place, maybe it's just home. Everyone agrees, in principle, that a buyout is the fair answer.

Then someone asks the question that actually matters: bought out at what price? And that's where the agreement usually stops.

If you're the personal representative or one of the heirs trying to work this out, here's how that number gets set in practice, and the places this quietly falls apart.

First, the disclaimer that has to come first: I'm a real estate agent, not an attorney or a CPA. Montana probate has its own rules about distribution, timing, and what a personal representative needs court approval to do, and those rules depend on the will and the specifics of the estate. What follows is meant to sharpen the questions you bring to your attorney and CPA, not to replace their answers.

## Whose number counts, and whose doesn't

Every sibling buyout I've seen struggle started with the same mistake: someone's opinion of value got treated as the value.

The sibling who wants to keep the house has every incentive to think it's worth less. The siblings being bought out have every incentive to think it's worth more. The county assessor's number reflects tax policy, not market value, and is often years behind. A sibling's memory of "what the Petersons got for their place down the road" isn't a comparable, it's a rumor.

None of those numbers can settle a disagreement, because none of them are neutral. The fix is the same one that works in every estate sale: an independent, licensed appraisal — often the same date-of-death appraisal the estate needs for tax purposes anyway. It isn't a perfect substitute for what the house would fetch on the open market, but it's defensible, it's in writing, and it's nobody's opinion. Get it before anyone states a number out loud. Positions harden fast once a figure is on the table, even an informal one.

## How the buying sibling actually pays for it

Once there's a value, the next fight is usually about how the buyout gets financed — and this is where I see families lose months.

The sibling keeping the house typically needs to refinance the inherited property into a new mortgage in their own name, using the loan proceeds to pay the other heirs their share. That means qualifying on their own income and their own debts, with a fresh appraisal from a lender who has zero interest in the estate's peace and quiet. A number the family agreed on informally can come in lower — or higher — once a bank's appraiser looks at the same house. Confirm financing capacity in week one, not after everyone has emotionally settled on a figure. I've watched a sibling agree to "buy the others out," spend two months telling the family it was handled, and then fail to qualify for the loan — which restarts the entire negotiation from a much angrier starting point.

## The costs that don't exist in a normal sale

A sibling buyout isn't a normal purchase, and pricing it like one skips real costs that need to be assigned somewhere.

There's no listing agent commission, but there's often a new appraisal fee, refinance closing costs, title work, and sometimes a small transfer or recording cost depending on how the deed moves. Someone has to decide whether those come off the top of the buyout price, get split evenly among all heirs, or land entirely on the sibling keeping the house. There's no universal answer — it's a negotiation — but it needs to be decided explicitly and in writing, not assumed. The estates that avoid resentment later are the ones where this got settled before the closing, not argued about after.

## When the appraisal comes in lower than someone expected

This happens more than families expect, and it's worth planning for before it does. If the independent appraisal lands below what a sibling was counting on, the instinct is to argue the number. Resist that. The more useful conversation is what the family does *with* the number: does the buyout proceed at the appraised value, does the family get a second opinion, does the sibling staying in the house need to bring cash to the table to make the others whole. Decide the process for handling a disappointing number before you have one — it's a much calmer conversation in the abstract than it is once a specific figure is sitting in front of everyone.

## Probate puts a clock on all of this

Montana probate timelines add real pressure here that a normal home sale doesn't have. Depending on the type of probate and the estate's obligations, there can be deadlines for distributing assets or closing out the estate that don't wait for siblings to reach comfortable consensus on financing. That's exactly why I push families to get the appraisal and the financing conversation moving in parallel, early, rather than sequentially — waiting for one before starting the other is often what turns a manageable few months into a year.

So here's the question worth asking before the next family conversation: has anyone actually gotten an independent number yet, or is everyone still negotiating from a guess?
