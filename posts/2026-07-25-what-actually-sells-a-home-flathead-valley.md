# What Actually Sells a Home in the Flathead Valley Right Now

If you're thinking about selling your home in Kalispell or Whitefish this fall, here's a question worth sitting with before you talk to anyone about a list price: *do you know what the market will actually pay for your home — or do you know what you'd like it to pay?*

Most sellers can't honestly answer that. And in this market, the gap between those two numbers is where deals go to die.

Here's where we are, as of the June 2026 data (source: Movoto/MLS, June 2026): 732 active listings in Kalispell, a median list price of $627,495, and a median of 58 days on market. Zillow's index has the typical Kalispell home value essentially flat over the past year — up just 0.2% as of late May 2026. That's not a crash. It's also not 2021. It's a normal market — and normal markets are unforgiving to sellers who wing it.

In my experience, three things sell a home in conditions like these: pricing to the data, preparation before the sign goes up, and a marketing plan instead of a prayer. Let's take them one at a time.

## Pricing to the Data — Not to 2022

Hard truth: in a flat market, overpricing isn't ambitious. It's expensive.

Here's how it usually goes. A seller anchors on what a neighbor got at the peak, lists high "to leave room to negotiate," and then watches the market answer. And the market answers fast — your first two weeks generate the most showings you will ever get. Miss the price, and those buyers don't make lowball offers. They just don't come.

Then the price cuts start. And every cut tells the remaining buyers the same story: *something's wrong with this one, and the seller is getting nervous.* Chasing the market down almost always nets less than pricing right on day one — often meaningfully less, after the extra months of carrying costs.

Pricing to the data means looking at what has actually closed in your neighborhood in the last 90 days, adjusted for condition and season — not list prices, not Zestimates, not what your home "should" be worth. When I run a pricing brief for a seller, we look at the comps together, line by line, so the number isn't my opinion or yours. It's the market's.

Ask yourself: if your home got zero offers in the first 21 days, do you have a plan — or would you be improvising?

## Prep Before the Sign Goes Up

With 732 homes on the market, buyers in the Flathead Valley have selection. That means your home isn't competing against "the market" in the abstract — it's competing against the twelve other listings in your price band that a buyer will tour the same weekend.

The prep that moves the needle here isn't a full remodel. It's the unglamorous list:

- **Fix the small stuff first.** Dripping faucets, cracked outlet covers, the door that sticks. Buyers see one deferred item and mentally multiply it through the whole house — and so does their inspector.
- **Get ahead of the inspection.** In this market, more deals die in inspection than in negotiation. If your roof, well, septic, or heating system has a known issue, deciding how to handle it *before* listing keeps you in control instead of reacting under deadline.
- **Neutralize and lighten.** Mountain homes in our valley sell on light and views. Anything blocking a window or darkening a room is costing you money.
- **Stage for the life buyers are moving here for.** You don't need a staging company for every home, but the gear wall of skis and the mudroom that handles a Montana winter? Shown right, those are selling points, not clutter.

My rule with sellers is simple: we don't put the sign in the yard until the home is ready for its best two weeks. You only get one first impression, and in a 58-day market you can afford the week of prep far more than you can afford a stale listing.

## A Marketing Plan Instead of a Prayer

The third piece is the one most sellers never see until it's too late: what actually happens after the listing goes live.

"We'll put it on the MLS and hold an open house" is not a plan. That's the minimum. A real plan for this market looks more like a mission brief — because that's how I learned to run anything with real stakes: objective, phases, timeline, contingencies.

Concretely, that means professional photography scheduled before launch day, not after. Pricing and launch timed to the week, because buyer traffic in the Flathead Valley is seasonal and predictable. A defined showing window to concentrate early interest. A pre-planned response for each scenario at day 21 — multiple offers, one offer, or silence — decided calmly in advance, not emotionally under pressure.

You should know, before your home hits the market, exactly what happens in week one, week three, and week six. If an agent can't show you that plan in writing, you're not hiring a plan. You're hiring a prayer.

## The Bottom Line for Flathead Valley Sellers

The sellers winning in Kalispell and Whitefish right now aren't the ones with the highest list prices. They're the ones who treated the sale like it deserved a plan: priced to the data, prepped before launch, marketed with intent.

So here's the question to reflect on: *if you listed in the next 60 days, which of those three would you be least ready for?*
