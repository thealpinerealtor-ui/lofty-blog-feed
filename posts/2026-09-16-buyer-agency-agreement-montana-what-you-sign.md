# What You Sign Before You Tour: Buyer Agency in Montana

Most buyers sign it on a doorstep. Phone in one hand, agent's tablet in the other, ninety seconds before the first showing of the day — and most of them don't read a line of it before they do.

That's the buyer representation agreement, and since the nationwide practice changes that followed the 2024 NAR settlement, it's not optional paperwork anymore. If you want an agent to walk you through a house that's listed with a Montana Regional MLS participant, you sign one first. So if you're about to start touring Kalispell or Whitefish homes and someone hands you a form before you've seen a single closet, here's what it actually says — and what to look at before your name goes on it.

## The Document at the Door

A buyer representation agreement is a written contract between you and a real estate agent (or the agent's brokerage) that spells out the working relationship before any house-hunting starts. It's the buyer-side mirror of the listing agreement a seller signs with their agent. Neither is new in concept — what changed in 2024 is that it's now required in writing, signed, before a buyer's agent can tour a property with you.

That's a reasonable thing to require. A written scope of work is how you hold someone to a standard — what they're doing for you, how long, and on what terms. The problem isn't the requirement. The problem is signing it at the door without understanding what's in it.

## Term and Exclusivity: What You're Actually Agreeing To

Two questions matter more than the rest of the page combined.

**How long does it run?** Some agreements run 90 days, some run a year. If this is your first time working with an agent, there's no reason to sign anything longer than a single showing or a short trial window — 30 days is common and reasonable to ask for. You can always extend it once you know the agent is a fit. Locking into a long term before the first tour is backwards.

**Is it exclusive?** An exclusive agreement means that agent represents you for the term, full stop — even if you stumble across a house on your own, at an open house, or through a friend's listing. A non-exclusive agreement lets you work with more than one agent at a time. Most buyers don't need multiple agents; they need to know which kind they signed. If you find a for-sale-by-owner or a new-construction spec home on a Sunday drive while under an exclusive agreement, that agent may still be owed a role — or a fee — depending on how the form is written. Ask before it happens, not after.

## Scope and Compensation

The agreement also sets the territory: one specific property, a single town, or the whole Flathead Valley. If you're not sure yet whether you're buying in Kalispell proper or looking as far out as Columbia Falls or Bigfork, don't sign something that boxes you into one zip code before you've decided.

Then there's the part that changed the most in the last two years: how the agent gets paid, and who pays it. Buyer-agent compensation is no longer something that gets quietly handled through the MLS listing. The agreement states a specific number or percentage, and it states who's expected to pay it — the seller (through the offer), the buyer directly, or some combination negotiated deal by deal. None of that is fixed by law or by MLS rule anymore. It's a line in the document you're signing, and it's negotiable. Read it, and if the number doesn't match what you expected going in, ask about it before you're three showings deep.

## The Protection Period

Most agreements include a protection period — a window, often 30 to 90 days after the agreement ends, during which the agent is still owed compensation if you buy a home they introduced you to or showed you during the term. This exists so an agent who does the work of showing you fifteen houses isn't cut out of the deal because you waited three weeks to make an offer. It's standard, and it's fair in principle. What matters is knowing the window exists and roughly how long it runs, so there's no surprise if you go back to a house you saw with one agent after you've started working with another.

## If It Isn't Working

A buyer agreement isn't a life sentence. If the relationship isn't working — slow communication, a mismatch in how you search, an agent who isn't showing up prepared — most agreements have a cancellation path, and a good agent won't make you fight for it. Ask what that path looks like before you sign, not after you're frustrated. If it isn't spelled out, ask directly: "If this isn't a fit, how do we end it?" The answer to that question tells you a lot about who you're about to spend the next few months with.

None of this is a hurdle standing between you and the house you want. It's the tool that makes sure the person walking you through it is actually working for you, on terms you both agreed to and can both point back to. Read it before you sign it, ask the questions above, and the rest of the search gets a lot simpler.

*This post explains general practice and is not legal advice. Buyer agency forms vary by brokerage — review the specific agreement you're asked to sign, and consult an attorney if you have questions about its terms.*
