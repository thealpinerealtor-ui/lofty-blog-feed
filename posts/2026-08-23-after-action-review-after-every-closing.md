# The After-Action Review: What Happens After Every Closing

Here's a question most people never think to ask the professionals they hire: after the job is done, does anybody sit down and go over what went wrong?

Not what went right. Everyone remembers that part. What *wobbled*. What almost cost you something. What nobody caught until it was nearly too late.

In the Army, and later in protective services work for the US government, an operation never ended at the objective. It ended at the debrief. You sat down while it was still fresh — sometimes filthy, usually tired, occasionally still keyed up — and you were honest about it. Not to assign blame. Because the next one depended on it.

Every closing I do in Kalispell and Whitefish gets the same treatment. Nobody asks me to. The client is already moved in and thinking about paint colors. But the file gets read one more time, on purpose, and here's why that matters to whoever hires me next.

## What an after-action review actually is

The military version is unglamorous and it works: four questions, asked in order, with rank left at the door.

What was supposed to happen. What actually happened. Why there's a gap between those two things. What changes next time.

That last one is the whole point. A debrief that ends in a story is just a story. A debrief that ends in a changed procedure is an investment. The reason the format survives is that it converts one team's expensive lesson into everybody else's cheap one.

Real estate has almost no version of this. Deals close, commissions get paid, and the agent moves to the next file carrying nothing forward but a vague sense that inspections have been rough lately. Every buyer and seller pays full tuition for the same lessons, over and over, because nobody wrote anything down.

## The four questions I ask after every Flathead closing

**Where did this deal nearly come apart?** Every transaction has a moment. Sometimes it's the appraisal. Sometimes it's a lender who went quiet for six days. Sometimes it's a message sent at the wrong hour to the wrong party. I write down what the moment was, what the early warning looked like, and whether I saw it coming or got told about it.

**What did the inspection turn up that I should have anticipated?** This one stings the most and pays the best. Older Flathead homes have patterns. Certain build eras, certain roof pitches, certain well and septic configurations, certain drainage situations on certain slopes — they repeat. Every inspection report I read closely makes the next walkthrough sharper. By the time I'm standing in a house with a client, a decent share of what a report will eventually say is already on my radar, because I've read a lot of reports on houses just like it and I took notes.

**What did the negotiation reveal about the market that week?** Not what the headlines said. What the other side actually did. Where sellers held, where they folded, what concessions were moving, how long real buyers were waiting before they wrote. Aggregate market data is a month behind by the time you read it. The behavior of the last three people across the table from me is current.

**What do I change?** If the answer is "nothing," I didn't look hard enough.

## What this changes for you, concretely

A few anonymized examples of what debriefs have actually altered in how I work — details changed, but the lessons are real:

A financing contingency nearly expired because a lender's document request came through on a Friday afternoon and sat unanswered over a weekend. Nothing was lost. But the review changed the procedure: financing timelines now get a mid-week check-in from me, not a deadline-week scramble, and my clients know before they sign what the lender is likely to ask for and when.

A seller and I set a price we both believed in, and the first ten days of showings said something different. The debrief question wasn't "was the price wrong." It was "what did the first week tell us that we didn't act on fast enough." Now I brief sellers up front on exactly what the first fourteen days of feedback mean and what we do about it, before there's any emotion attached to the number.

An inspection surfaced a drainage issue that, in hindsight, the lot's grade had been advertising the entire time. That's now a thing I look at before we write, not after.

None of that is dramatic. That's the point. Discipline is a series of unremarkable adjustments that add up to a transaction where fewer things are surprises.

## Why clients feel this as calm

Here's what I've noticed: clients almost never see the preparation. What they experience is the absence of chaos.

The contingency was already written before it was needed. The comps were run before the question got asked. The file was read before the call. When the inspection report lands and it's ugly, nobody has to panic, because the response is a procedure instead of a reaction.

That calm isn't a personality trait, and it isn't a temperament I was born with. It's the residue of a lot of debriefs. It's what happens when the last twenty transactions were treated as training for this one.

So here's the question worth sitting with, whether you're buying, selling, or just thinking about it: when things get complicated in your transaction — and in a market like this one, something will — do you want someone who's reacting for the first time, or someone who's already been through the debrief?
