# The Step-Up Basis: What Selling an Inherited Montana Home Really Costs

Here's the question I hear most often from heirs, usually asked quietly, sometimes with a little dread behind it: *"If we sell Mom's house, how much are we going to lose to taxes?"*

And here's what almost nobody has explained to them before they ask: usually far less than they fear. Often close to nothing.

The reason is a tax rule called the **step-up in basis** — and if you've inherited a home in Kalispell, Whitefish, or anywhere in the Flathead Valley, it may be the single most important piece of the picture. It's also the one most families learn about last.

First, the required disclaimer, and I mean it: I'm a real estate agent, not a CPA or an attorney. What follows is general information to help you ask better questions. Your specific numbers need to come from your tax professional.

## What "basis" means — and why it resets

When you sell a property, capital gains tax is calculated on the difference between what you sell it for and your *basis* — roughly, what was paid for it, plus certain improvements.

Now think about a Flathead Valley home your parents bought decades ago. A house purchased in Kalispell in 1992 for $110,000 might be worth $600,000 today. If your parents sold it themselves, that gap is the gain the tax math starts from.

But when a property passes to heirs, something different happens. In most cases, the basis *resets* — it "steps up" — to the property's fair market value on the date of death.

Read that again, because it changes everything: the decades of appreciation that happened during your parents' lifetime generally aren't taxed as your gain when you inherit. Your starting line moves to the value on the day they passed.

So if the home was worth $600,000 at the date of death and the estate sells it a few months later for $615,000, the taxable gain isn't $505,000. It's in the neighborhood of $15,000 — before selling costs, which reduce it further. For many families, once commissions and closing costs come out, the taxable gain on a reasonably prompt sale is small or effectively zero.

That's the fear most heirs carry into this process, dissolved by one rule. Which is exactly why it frustrates me that so few families hear about it early.

## Why the date-of-death appraisal matters — even if you're not selling yet

Here's the practical catch: a step-up is only as good as your ability to *document* the stepped-up value.

That's what a date-of-death appraisal is for. A licensed appraiser values the property as of the date of death — not as of today, not as of whenever you get around to it. That number becomes the anchor for everything downstream: the estate's records, the eventual sale, and the tax return that follows.

Two things I tell every personal representative:

**Get it done early.** Appraisers can work retrospectively, but the further you get from the date of death — especially in a market that moves like the Flathead's — the harder the reconstruction becomes. An appraisal ordered promptly is clean evidence. One ordered three years later is an argument.

**Get it even if you plan to keep the house.** Some heirs hold the property for years — as a family cabin, a rental, an eventual retirement landing spot. The step-up still applies, but only the value at death gets the reset. Everything the home gains *after* that date is potential future gain, measured against the date-of-death number. If that number was never documented, you've made your future self's tax problem much harder than it needed to be.

One more nuance worth knowing: inherited property is generally treated as long-term for capital gains purposes, no matter how briefly you've held it. Sell six weeks after probate opens and the gain — if there is one — typically gets long-term treatment. Again: confirm with your CPA.

## The records a personal representative should gather now

If you're serving as personal representative of a Montana estate, here's the file I'd build before the house question even comes up. Think of it as pre-mission preparation — done early, it makes every later step calmer.

The date-of-death appraisal. Records of major improvements made after the date of death, since those can adjust the basis going forward. Every invoice from the sale process itself — repairs, cleanout, staging, commissions, closing costs — because selling expenses matter in the final math. Property tax and insurance records showing the estate maintained the home. And the probate paperwork establishing your authority to sell, which the title company will ask for anyway.

None of this is complicated. All of it is easier to collect in month one than in month fourteen — and your CPA and attorney will thank you for it.

## What this means for the timing decision

Families often assume they need to rush a sale "before taxes eat the value," or hold the property for some magic period. The step-up quietly removes most of that pressure. Because the basis resets at death, a prompt, orderly sale usually produces little taxable gain — and holding longer doesn't improve the tax picture, it just starts the clock on new appreciation.

Which means the real questions become practical ones: What does the home need before market? Who's handling the contents? How do out-of-state heirs stay informed? Those are solvable, one phase at a time — and they're a subject I've covered before and will again.
