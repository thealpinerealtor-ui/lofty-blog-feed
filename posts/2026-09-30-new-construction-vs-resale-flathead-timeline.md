# New Construction vs. Resale in the Flathead: What the Timeline Actually Looks Like

If you're cross-shopping a new-build lot against a resale house in Kalispell or Whitefish, chances are you've already run the price numbers three different ways. Here's the question most buyers don't ask until they're three months in: what does the calendar actually look like on each path?

Price gets compared line by line. Timeline usually doesn't — and timeline is where new construction quietly changes the whole plan, from your rate lock to your lease end date to whether you're moving in before or after the first hard freeze.

## The resale timeline, in plain numbers

A resale purchase in this market typically closes in 30 to 45 days from an accepted offer, assuming financing, appraisal, and inspection all move on schedule. That window is fairly predictable because the house already exists — there's no permitting, no framing schedule, no weather delay on a foundation pour. Your lender, title company, and inspector are working against a known structure, not a set of blueprints.

That predictability is the resale trade-off: you know the finish level, the layout, and the move-in date within a few days, almost from the moment your offer is accepted.

## What actually stretches a new-build timeline

New construction in Flathead County runs through a few checkpoints that a resale skips entirely, and each one is a place the calendar can slip:

**Permitting.** Depending on the jurisdiction and whether the lot is inside city limits or in the county, permit review can take anywhere from a few weeks to a couple of months before a builder can break ground. This is almost entirely outside your control and outside the builder's, too — it's queue-dependent on the permitting office.

**The builder's queue.** Even a builder you trust is working multiple jobs at once. Ask directly where your build sits in that queue and get a realistic start date in writing, not a verbal estimate. "We'll get to it this fall" is not a start date.

**Change orders.** Every upgrade, every "let's move that outlet," every substituted material because the original is backordered — each one can add both cost and calendar time. A single significant change order can push a close date by weeks, especially if it involves anything structural or requires re-ordering materials.

**Weather.** Flathead Valley winters are real. A foundation that should have been poured in September but gets pushed to November because of permitting delays is now racing the ground freeze, and that alone can add a season to the build.

Put together, a realistic new-construction timeline in this market often runs six months to well over a year from signed contract to move-in — compared to 30-45 days for a comparable resale. That's not a knock on new construction. It's the honest gap buyers need to plan around.

## Where a rate lock fits into this

If you're financing, your rate lock matters more on new construction than most buyers expect. A standard lock might run 30 to 60 days — nowhere close to covering a build that could take six months or longer. Builders often work with a lender who offers an extended or "lock-and-shop" product designed for construction timelines, but that's a conversation to have before you sign a builder contract, not after your original lock has already expired and you're staring at a higher rate.

Ask your lender directly: "What happens to my rate if this build runs past my lock window?" Get the answer in writing before you commit.

## HOA and covenant review — new developments vs. established neighborhoods

New developments often come with a fresh HOA, and "fresh" means the covenants, fees, and enforcement track record don't have years of history to review yet. Compare that to an established Kalispell or Whitefish neighborhood, where you can actually see how an HOA has behaved — its fee history, its reserve fund, how it's handled maintenance or disputes. On a new development, you're reviewing what's written on paper with less real-world track record to check it against. Read the covenants closely, and ask what's actually funded in the reserve account versus what's projected.

## So which one is right for you?

This isn't a case against new construction — plenty of buyers have very good reasons to want it: nothing to renovate, current building codes, a floor plan built around exactly how they live. The point is picking with your eyes open on time, not just price.

If you need to be in a home by a specific date — a lease ending, a school year starting, a job relocation — that alone might settle the question. If your timeline has real flexibility and you want a home built exactly to your spec, new construction might be worth the wait.

Where are you actually flexible on your move-in date, and where are you not? That question usually clarifies the decision faster than another round of price comparisons.
