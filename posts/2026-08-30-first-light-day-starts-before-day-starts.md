# First Light: Why the Day Starts Before the Day Starts

Here's a question worth asking about anyone you're thinking of hiring: what were they doing three hours before you called them?

Not in a nosy way. In a practical one. Because by the time you pick up the phone with a real question — an offer deadline, an inspection report that just landed, a lender who's gone quiet — the answer you get is already mostly determined. It was set hours earlier, in a window you'll never see.

I'm usually up and working before the valley is. Long before it, most days. People assume that's a fitness thing. The training is in there, and I do care about it. But it isn't the reason, and it never was.

## What the early hours were actually for

In the Army, and later in protective services work for the US government, the day got won or lost in the hours nobody watched.

Gear laid out the night before, then checked again cold, because the version of you at 0400 is not the version of you who packed at 2200. The route walked in your head twice — not the route as drawn, the route as it would actually go, including the part where it doesn't. The small hard thing done before anybody asked for it.

By the time anything actually happened, most of it had already been handled. That's the whole trick, and it isn't much of a trick. It's just front-loading. You move the thinking to the quiet part of the day so that the loud part of the day only requires execution.

Nobody gives you credit for that block of time. Nobody sees it. If it's done well, the visible part of the day looks almost boring, and that's the goal. Drama in the field usually means somebody skipped the morning.

## The part that isn't about fitness

Getting after it before sunrise in a Montana winter — fifteen below, dark, glare ice, no reason on earth to be out there — does not make me a better negotiator. That would be a nice story. It isn't true.

What it does is smaller and more useful. It settles the question of whether the day happens on my terms. By the time the sun comes up over the Swan Range, I've already done one thing I didn't feel like doing. The rest of the day is negotiating with someone who's already won the first argument.

That's the entire crossover. Anybody who tells you cold-water discipline translates directly into deal-making is selling something.

## What actually gets done before the phone rings

Here's the honest inventory of that early block, because "discipline" is a useless word if it doesn't turn into specifics.

**Active files get read before anything else.** Not skimmed for what's due. Read, looking for what's about to be due, and for the thing that's quietly gone three days without a response. Most transaction problems announce themselves well before they become problems. They just do it in the boring paperwork nobody reads on a Sunday.

**Deadlines get walked forward, not backward.** Inspection window, financing contingency, appraisal, title commitment, closing date. Which one is closest, who has the ball, and what happens to the other four if that one slips. If a date is going to be tight in nine days, I'd rather know about it this morning than the afternoon it breaks.

**The market gets checked while it's quiet.** New listings, price changes, what actually went pending overnight and at what number. Aggregated county data is weeks behind by the time it publishes. What sold in Kalispell or Whitefish this week is current, and it's only current if you look at it consistently.

**The hard call gets planned.** If I owe someone news they won't like — a low appraisal, a repair request that isn't going to fly, a price conversation a seller isn't ready for — I work out how to say it before I'm saying it. Sitting down, on purpose, in the quiet. Bad news delivered well is a different experience than bad news delivered off the cuff, and clients can absolutely tell the difference.

**The contingency gets written before it's needed.** This is the one that pays. What's plan B if the buyer's financing wobbles, if the inspection turns up something structural, if the other agent goes dark for a week. Not because I expect all of it. Because writing it down takes eleven minutes at six in the morning and takes a deal apart at four in the afternoon.

None of that is impressive. It's a routine. That's precisely why it works — impressive things are hard to repeat, and routines aren't.

## Why this is a service standard, not a personality trait

Discipline isn't a virtue I'm claiming here. It's a system I'm describing, and the reason it matters to you is that it separates an agent who's reacting to your transaction from one who's already run it. Anyone can be calm on an easy deal. The question is what happens when your file is the complicated one — a divorce sale where two households have to coordinate, an estate where the heirs live in three states, a closing where something genuinely goes wrong.

In those files, preparation isn't a nicety. It's the entire service.

What my clients experience isn't the early mornings. They never see that part. What they feel is that nothing catches me flat. The question they were nervous to ask has already been answered. When something ugly lands, the response is a procedure instead of a scramble.

That calm isn't temperament. It's the residue of a lot of quiet hours.

## The question worth sitting with

If you're weighing who to work with on the biggest transaction of your year — especially if your situation is complicated, because complicated is the file I want — the question isn't whether someone seems confident when you meet them. Everybody seems confident at the listing appointment.

The question is what they'll have already done, three hours before you call with the problem.
