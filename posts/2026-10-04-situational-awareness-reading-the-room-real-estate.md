# Situational Awareness: Reading the Room Before You Read the Contract

Here's a question worth sitting with before your next showing or negotiation: by the time a problem shows up in an email or a contract addendum, how long had it actually been building?

Almost always, longer than anyone wanted to admit.

## The habit of noticing before it's obvious

In a higher-stakes line of work, you learn to clock small deviations from baseline before they become anything worth reporting — a changed routine, a longer pause than usual, someone standing slightly off from where they normally would. None of it is dramatic in the moment. Most of it is forgettable. But the pattern underneath it tells you something is shifting before anyone says so out loud.

Real estate runs on the same signal, just lower stakes and friendlier terrain. A buyer who lingers an extra two minutes in the primary bedroom isn't nothing. A seller who answers a question about the roof a half-second too fast, or too carefully, isn't nothing either. Neither is the inspector who goes quiet near the crawlspace access instead of narrating like they did everywhere else. None of these are facts you can put in a report. All of them are worth paying attention to.

## Why this matters more than people think

Most negotiations don't fall apart over the big, obvious issue — the one everyone saw coming and priced in from day one. They fall apart over the thing nobody named early enough: a buyer who went quiet on a showing and nobody asked why, a seller who kept deflecting a specific question until it finally surfaced as a surprise during inspection, an agent on the other side whose tone shifted two emails before their client actually said they were walking.

By the time any of that lands in writing, it's already a problem to manage instead of a conversation to have. The gap between noticing and reacting is where deals either stay healthy or start quietly eroding.

## What this actually looks like in a transaction

This isn't about reading minds, and it isn't about being suspicious of everyone in the room. It's about treating a showing, a counteroffer, or a walkthrough as more than a checklist to get through. A few concrete habits:

**In a showing:** Watch where people slow down versus where they move through on autopilot. The room they circle back to twice is telling you something about priorities nobody stated out loud — useful information when it comes time to write or respond to an offer.

**In negotiation:** Notice when a response takes longer than the question warranted, or arrives faster than it should have. Both are data. Neither means panic — it means ask one more question before you answer theirs.

**In inspection and disclosure:** Pay attention to what someone volunteers without being asked versus what only comes out when pressed. A seller who brings up the well pump issue before the inspector finds it is a different risk profile than one who waits to see if it gets flagged.

**In communication cadence:** A buyer or seller who normally replies within hours and suddenly takes two days isn't automatically a lost deal — but it's a cue to check in directly instead of assuming silence means disinterest or, worse, assuming it means nothing at all.

None of this replaces the paperwork, the inspection report, or the numbers. It's what happens in the space before those things, where most of the actual leverage and actual risk quietly live.

## The part people get backwards

The instinct for a lot of buyers and sellers is to wait for something to become official before reacting to it — to treat a hunch as not worth mentioning until there's a document to point to. That's backwards. The document is the end of the story, not the beginning of it. By the time an issue is in writing, the only options left are damage control. Caught two weeks earlier, in a conversation instead of a contract clause, it's just a Tuesday.

This is also why a transaction benefits from having someone in it whose job is specifically to be paying attention the whole way through — not reactively, after a text message lands with bad news, but continuously, from the first showing to the final walkthrough. That's not a special talent. It's a trained habit, and it transfers cleanly from rooms with a lot more on the line than a Flathead Valley listing.

## A question worth sitting with

Think back to the last negotiation, personal or professional, that went sideways. Was there a moment — a pause, a change in tone, a question that got answered a little too smoothly — that you noticed at the time and set aside, only to watch it turn into the actual issue two weeks later? Most people can name the moment once they look for it. The goal isn't hindsight. It's catching that moment while there's still time to do something useful with it.
