# The First Price Cut: How Long to Wait, and How Much

Every seller gets the same advice when a listing goes quiet: give it more time. Don't panic. The right buyer is out there.

That advice isn't wrong, exactly. It's just incomplete, and in the current Kalispell and Whitefish market, incomplete advice gets expensive. The sellers I watch close nearest to their actual number aren't the ones who held their price the longest. They're the ones who moved once, early, and moved enough to matter.

## Two Different Questions, One Bad Habit

"Should I cut my price?" isn't really one question. It's two — *how long do I wait* and *how much do I cut* — and most sellers answer both of them by feel instead of by evidence. They wait because waiting feels safer than admitting the number might be wrong. Then, when they finally move, they cut just enough to feel like they did something, which is usually the worst amount: too small to reach a new pool of buyers, too public not to signal that the house is struggling.

Treat them as two separate decisions, each with its own answer, and the whole process gets a lot less emotional.

## How Long to Wait

The honest answer is shorter than most sellers want to hear, and it's shorter than it was a year ago. Earlier this week I wrote about Kalispell's median days on market stretching to 77, up from 62 a year ago (source: Movoto Kalispell market data, period through August 2026, page updated September 21, 2026). That number isn't just a market snapshot — it's the clock every listing is actually running against now, whether the seller has looked at it or not.

Here's what that means in practice: your first two to three weeks on market are the only period where you're seeing your true audience. Every buyer actively searching your price band and area sees a new listing in that window — saved searches fire, agents preview, the algorithm gives you its best push. That audience doesn't come back a second time with the same energy. If showings are thin and the ones you get don't return for a second look in that window, that's not a sign to wait longer. It's the answer.

Waiting past three weeks with no meaningful activity doesn't buy you more information — it buys you a listing that's aged into "still here" on a buyer's search, competing against houses that are new. At 77 days as the current median, a house that's already sitting at day 30 with no traction isn't halfway through a normal process. It's telling you something the calendar can't fix.

## What a Too-Small Cut Actually Signals

This is where sellers lose the most value without realizing it. A five-thousand-dollar trim on a $650,000 listing doesn't reach a single new buyer — most search filters work in round increments, and a cut that doesn't cross one of those thresholds is invisible to anyone who isn't already watching your listing.

But it isn't invisible to the people who are watching. Agents tracking the MLS see a small reduction and read it correctly: a seller testing the water, expecting to cut again. Buyers who've already toured and passed see the same thing and wait, betting there's another cut coming before they need to act. A small first cut doesn't create urgency. It advertises that patience will be rewarded — which means the next offer you get, if one comes at all, tends to come in low, anchored to the number you'll probably drop to next.

The sellers who cut once and move on are the ones who size that first cut to actually change who sees the house, not just to move the number on the listing sheet.

## Sizing the Cut Off Comparables, Not a Round Number

The fix isn't cutting bigger for the sake of it — it's cutting off the right information. Pull what's actually closed within roughly a mile in the last 60 to 90 days, not what's currently listed and not what a neighbor claims they were offered in conversation. Closings are evidence. Everything else is a guess dressed up as a data point.

Once you have that number, size your reduction to land your listing in front of a different search bracket entirely — below a round threshold buyers actually filter on, not a few thousand dollars under where you started. A house that moves from $749,000 to $739,000 hasn't changed who sees it. The same house moving to $725,000 has. One clean move that crosses a real line does more than three small ones that never do, and it costs you less in perception each time, because there's only one move to explain instead of a pattern to defend.

## The Practical Version

If you're inside your first three weeks, watch traffic and second visits, not the calendar. If you're past that window with thin activity, the question isn't whether to move — it's how far, measured against what's actually closed nearby, not what feels fair. One deliberate cut, sized correctly and made early, beats three apologetic ones every time.

So here's the question worth sitting with if your listing has gone quiet: are you waiting because the data says wait, or because moving the number feels like admitting something you'd rather not?
