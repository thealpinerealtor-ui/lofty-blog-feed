# Reading an Offer: What Sellers Look At After the Price

Two offers land on the same Kalispell listing within a day of each other. One is $18,000 higher. Most sellers open that one first, mentally accept it, and only skim the second. By the time the higher offer starts wobbling — a financing contingency that's tighter than it looked, an inspection window wide enough to renegotiate the price twice — the decision has usually already been made, and unwinding it costs weeks.

If you've ever wondered why a listing "in escrow" quietly reappears on the market a month later, this is usually why. The highest number on the page loses to a cleaner offer more often than sellers expect. Here's what actually determines whether an offer closes, in the order it's worth looking at.

## Financing Type and What a Preapproval Letter Actually Promises

A cash offer and a conventional loan offer aren't the same offer even at an identical price — cash removes appraisal risk and loan-underwriting risk entirely. Below that, a preapproval letter is not a guarantee; it's a lender's statement that a buyer looked qualified on the information given at the time. Ask who issued it — a national call-center lender and a local Flathead Valley lender don't carry the same weight with a listing agent who's watched deals from both fall apart or sail through. A preapproval that's more than 60–90 days old, or one with unusually broad conditions, is worth a phone call to the loan officer before you get attached to that number.

## Earnest Money as a Measure of Commitment

Earnest money isn't a fee — it's the buyer's skin in the game, and the size of it tells you something the purchase price doesn't. A token earnest deposit on a $650,000 Whitefish home is a signal, whether or not the buyer means it that way. Larger earnest money, especially with shorter contingency-removal deadlines attached to it, tends to come from buyers who've already decided this is the house. It also matters for what happens if the deal falls apart — what's refundable, what isn't, and under which contingencies, which is a conversation for your listing agent and the purchase agreement, not a guess.

## Inspection Window and Appraisal-Gap Language

A long, open-ended inspection period is where an accepted offer quietly turns into a second round of price negotiation two weeks later. A shorter, clearly scoped window — or an as-is offer with a defined inspection-for-information-only clause — tells you the buyer isn't planning to use inspection as a re-trade tool. Appraisal-gap language matters more in a market where values have softened: a buyer who's committed to covering some or all of a gap between contract price and a low appraisal is worth materially more than one who hasn't addressed it at all, even at a slightly lower offer price.

## Closing Date, Rent-Back Requests, and Who's Asking for What

Run the closing date against your own next move, not just the calendar. A 30-day close that leaves you scrambling for temporary housing can cost more in stress and moving expense than the $10,000 you gained on price. If a buyer is asking for a rent-back after closing so they can finish their own move, that's a negotiable point, not a red flag — but it needs terms in writing: daily rate, duration, and what happens if you're not out on schedule. Requests for seller-paid closing costs should be read against the net number, not the sticker price — a buyer asking for $8,000 in closing cost credit on top of a strong offer may still net you more than a lower, cleaner-looking number that carries no requests at all.

## Comparing Two Offers Side by Side

Build a simple table before you decide anything: price, financing type and lender, earnest money amount and refundability, inspection window length, appraisal-gap terms, closing date against your own timeline, and any credits or rent-back requests. Laid out that way, the "obvious" choice is often less obvious than the headline price made it look. This is also where a buyer's own home-sale contingency belongs on the list — a buyer whose purchase depends on their current house selling first is carrying risk that a same-price, contingency-free offer isn't.

None of this replaces your listing agent walking the actual contract language with you line by line — financing conditions, contingency deadlines, and gap-coverage clauses should be confirmed against the specific purchase agreement in front of you, not a general framework.

If you're comparing offers right now, or getting ready to list and want to know what a strong offer looks like in this market before the first one arrives, that's exactly the kind of conversation worth having early.
