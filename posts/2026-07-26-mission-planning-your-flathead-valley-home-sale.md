# Mission Planning a Home Sale: What the Army Taught Me About Real Estate

Have you ever sat across from a professional — an agent, a contractor, an advisor — and had the quiet feeling they were making it up as they went?

Most people have. And here's the uncomfortable part: in real estate, "winging it" is common enough that plenty of buyers and sellers assume it's normal. An agent lists the house, hopes for offers, reacts to whatever happens next.

In the Army, we had a name for operating that way. It was called getting people hurt.

I spent years as a US Army infantryman and later in high-stakes government security work, and one habit from that world runs through everything I do in real estate today: **you never execute without a brief.** Objective. Phases. Contingencies. Roles. Before anyone moves, everyone knows what we're doing, why, in what order, and what happens if things go sideways.

When six figures of your equity and your family's next chapter are on the line, I'd argue you deserve the same standard. Here's what that actually looks like when it's applied to buying or selling a home in the Flathead Valley.

## Start With the Objective — the Real One

Every mission brief starts with the objective, stated plainly. Not "do good things out there." Something you can measure.

Real estate is no different, and this is where most transactions drift off course before they start. "Sell my house" is not an objective. *"Net enough from the Kalispell house to buy in the school district we want, and be moved before the semester starts"* — that's an objective. So is *"sell the family property with the least friction between the heirs"* or *"buy our first home in Whitefish without wiping out the emergency fund."*

When the real objective is on the table, every decision downstream gets easier. Pricing, timing, which offer to take, what to fix and what to leave — you're no longer guessing, you're measuring options against the mission.

If your agent has never asked you what a win actually looks like for your family, that's worth noticing.

## Break It Into Phases

No one briefs a mission as one giant blob of activity. It's phases — each with its own tasks, timeline, and criteria for moving to the next.

A home sale phases out cleanly:

**Phase 1 — Preparation.** Pricing analysis against current Flathead Valley data, repairs and prep that actually return money, photography, pre-listing paperwork. This phase is where sales are won, and it happens before a single buyer sees the house.

**Phase 2 — Launch and marketing.** The listing goes live with a plan behind it — not "post it and pray." First two weeks of showing activity are your best market intelligence; a disciplined agent is reading that feedback and reporting it to you.

**Phase 3 — Negotiation and contract.** Offers evaluated against your objective, not just the top-line number. Terms, timelines, financing strength, contingencies.

**Phase 4 — Contract to close.** Inspection, appraisal, title, lender milestones — each with a date and a responsible party. This is where "we'll figure it out" transactions fall apart, because nobody was tracking the checkpoints.

Buyers get the same structure, just different phases: financing and pre-approval, search and showing strategy, offer, and closing. Either way, at any given moment you should be able to answer one question: *what phase are we in, and what has to happen to advance?*

## Plan the Contingencies Before You Need Them

Here's the part of mission planning civilians underestimate: most of the brief isn't about the plan working. It's about what we do when it doesn't.

What if the inspection turns up a surprise on the well or septic — common enough in our valley? What if the appraisal comes in light? What if the buyer's financing wobbles a week before closing? What if you get no offers in the first three weeks?

None of those should be discovered in the moment. Each one has a pre-planned response: a decision point, options already discussed, and an agreed threshold for walking away. When you've talked through the contingencies in advance, bad news becomes a branch in the plan instead of a crisis. That's the difference between a stressful transaction and a managed one — the surprises were already on the whiteboard.

## Everyone Knows Their Role

The last piece of any brief: roles. Who does what, and who makes which call.

In a transaction, that means clarity from day one. What I handle: pricing strategy, marketing, negotiation, coordinating the lender, title company, and inspectors, and keeping every deadline visible. What you own: the decisions — because it's your money and your family. My job is to make sure you never make one of those decisions without complete information and a clear recommendation.

That structure matters most when circumstances are complicated — a divorce sale where two parties need a neutral, disciplined process, or an estate sale with heirs in three states. The more emotion in the room, the more the process has to carry the weight.

## The Question Worth Asking

So here's the reflection I'd leave you with, whether you're eyeing Kalispell homes for sale or thinking about listing in Whitefish this fall:

*If you asked your agent today what phase your transaction is in, what the next checkpoint is, and what the contingency plan is if it slips — would you get an answer, or a pause?*
