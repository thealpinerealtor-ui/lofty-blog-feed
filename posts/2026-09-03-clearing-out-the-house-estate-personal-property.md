# Clearing Out the House: The Part Nobody Plans For

The legal side of an estate comes with a checklist. Somebody hands you one — file this, notify these people, open that account.

The garage doesn't come with anything.

In my experience, families almost never stall on the paperwork. They stall in the house. Forty years of things, no order to any of it, and nobody wanting to be the one who throws out something that turns out to have mattered. Months go by that way, and the estate pays for every one of them — taxes, insurance, utilities, a lawn somebody has to mow.

If you can feel that stall coming, here's a sequence that works.

The disclaimer first, and I mean it: I'm a real estate agent, not an attorney. What a personal representative may distribute, sell, or dispose of — and when — depends on the will and on Montana probate rules. Confirm your authority with your attorney before anything leaves the house. What follows is the order of operations, not the law.

## Documents and valuables come out first

Before a box gets donated, one person walks the house looking for two things: paper and portable value.

Paper means deeds, titles, insurance policies, tax returns, military discharge records, safe-deposit keys, and the handwritten note in a drawer that turns out to be a codicil. Portable value means jewelry, firearms, coins, cash tucked in books, and anything small enough to disappear during a clear-out without anyone noticing.

Do this early, with a second person present, and put it all in one secured place. Not because anyone in your family is a thief — but because a dozen people are about to move through that house, and the version of this story that ends badly always starts with "we found it later, we think." Firearms have their own transfer rules; ask your attorney before handing one to a nephew.

## Then sort into five piles, not two

Families freeze because they sort into "keep" and "toss," forcing an emotional verdict on every object. That's exhausting by lunch. Five categories move faster:

**Keep** — a specific person has claimed it and will physically carry it out.

**Distribute** — spoken for, but the person is in Tucson until October. This pile needs a shipping plan or a date, or it becomes a permanent resident.

**Sell** — real resale value: furniture in good condition, tools, equipment, collections, anything with a brand name people search for.

**Donate** — usable, but not worth the cost of selling. Most of the house lands here.

**Haul** — broken, stained, expired, or waterlogged. Be honest about the volume; it's usually a dumpster's worth, and renting one on day one beats eleven trips to the transfer station.

One rule that saves families grief: photograph anything sentimental before it goes. Nobody misses the object as much as not having looked at it one more time.

## How estate-sale companies actually work

The economics surprise people, so know them before you call anyone.

An estate-sale company runs the sale in the house over a weekend and takes a percentage of gross proceeds — commonly 30 to 50 percent, depending on the company, the size of the sale, and the labor the setup requires. Many have a minimum in sellable contents before they'll take the job. Ask directly: what's the split, is there a minimum, who handles what doesn't sell, and is there a separate cleanout fee.

Consignment is a different animal — the piece sits on their floor until it sells and you're paid on a delay. Fine for a few valuable items, bad for volume. Auction houses earn a call when there's a real collection. And "buyout" companies offer a flat number for the whole contents and haul it themselves; you'll net less, but when heirs are scattered and the calendar is the real problem, that can be the right trade — just make it knowingly.

Get two opinions before you commit, and get the terms in writing.

## When the heirs are in three states

This is the Flathead reality more often than not — one heir here, the others in Seattle and Dallas with a long weekend each.

What works: an inventory before anybody flies in. Walk the house with a phone, photograph rooms and then anything a person might care about, and share the album. Then set a claiming window — everyone marks what they want by a stated date, conflicts resolve by a method agreed on in advance, and silence counts as no claim. Rotating draft picks sounds childish until you watch it end a standoff in twenty minutes.

The visit itself is for goodbyes and boxes. Discovery by group, in person, is how weekends turn into arguments.

## Time the clear-out against the listing, not after it

Two failure modes, opposite directions. Photographing a full house and hoping buyers see past it costs real money — clutter reads as a project regardless of the bones. The other extreme is worse than people expect: a house stripped bare in October and listed in February has spent four months empty, aging in the eyes of the market, while the estate pays the carrying costs.

The version that works is staged. Valuables secured. Sale or donation of contents. Broom-clean and light repairs. Photos, then market — the last two close enough together that the house isn't sitting vacant waiting on a photographer.

And you don't have to empty it before I can tell you what it's worth. A walkthrough with the contents still in place is usually more useful — I can see the house instead of imagining it.

## The part worth saying out loud

The clear-out is the last thing the family does in that house together. Treat it like it matters — but put a calendar around it, or grief and logistics braid into a project nobody finishes.

So the question worth sitting with: is the hold-up actually the stuff — or that nobody has said out loud what date this needs to be done by?
