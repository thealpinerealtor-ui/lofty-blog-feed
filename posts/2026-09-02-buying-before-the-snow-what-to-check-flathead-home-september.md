# Buying Before the Snow: What to Check on a Flathead Home in September

Here's a question for anyone touring homes in the valley right now: what would this house look like on the second week of January, at ten below, with two feet on the roof?

Because a house shown in September tells you almost nothing about how it lives in February. The lawn is green, the driveway is dry, the furnace hasn't run in months, and every window is open. Everything that makes a Flathead winter easy or miserable is invisible in good weather.

The upside is that September is exactly when you can still get up on the roof, into the crawlspace, and down the road to see what you're buying. That window closes fast. Here's what to check while it's open.

## Start With the Roof — and Ask About Ice

Roof age is the first number to get, and "it's fine" is not an answer. Ask for the year it was replaced and who did it. If nobody knows, that's information too.

Then look at the pitch and the eaves. Low-slope roofs and complicated rooflines with valleys and dormers hold snow longer and are more prone to ice damming — that cycle where heat escaping the house melts snow on the roof, it refreezes at the cold eave, and water backs up under the shingles. Evidence of past damming shows up as stained or bubbled ceiling drywall near exterior walls and heat-cable staples along the roof edge. Heat cables aren't a red flag by themselves, but they're a sign someone was managing a problem rather than solving it.

Look at where the downspouts dump, too. Water directed at the foundation is a summer nuisance and a winter wall of ice.

## The Attic Is Where Winter Problems Come From

Most ice damming and a lot of heating cost start in the attic, not on the roof. Ask the inspector to actually get in there, not just look through the hatch.

What matters: insulation depth and whether it's even, or whether it's been pushed around and compressed. Whether soffit and ridge vents are present and not buried under insulation. Any sign of moisture — dark staining on the underside of the sheathing, frost residue lines, mold — which points to warm, damp house air getting into a cold attic. Bathroom fans venting into the attic instead of outside are a common find in older valley homes — a cheap fix compared to the sheathing they'll eventually rot.

## What Heats It — and What Backs That Up

This is the question I'd push hardest on, and it has two parts: what's the primary heat, and what happens when it fails or when the power goes out on a cold night?

**Propane.** Ask who owns the tank. A leased tank usually ties you to that supplier and their pricing; an owned tank lets you shop. Ask for fill history so you can see real winter consumption, not a guess. And confirm the tank isn't sitting where the plow will hit it or the roof will dump onto it.

**Wood stove.** Get the model and the install date, and have the inspector check clearances to combustibles, the hearth, and the chimney. Ask when the chimney was last swept — your insurer will ask about the stove anyway.

**Heat pump.** Ask specifically whether it's a cold-climate rated unit and what the backup heat source is. Standard air-source heat pumps lose capacity as temperatures fall; a cold-climate model or a strip-heat or furnace backup is what actually carries the house through a January cold snap. If the answer is "the heat pump handles it," ask what the electric bill looked like last February.

**Furnace or boiler.** Age, last service date, and — for gas — whether there's a carbon monoxide detector on every level.

And for any of the above: is there a generator hookup, or a plan for one?

## Where the Pipes Run

Frozen pipes are the winter emergency that costs the most and gets the least attention on a September walkthrough.

The questions: are there water lines running through an unheated crawlspace, garage, or exterior wall? How is the crawlspace insulated, and are the vents closed for winter? Is there heat tape on exposed runs, and does it work? Where's the main shutoff?

A crawlspace with standing water, bare dirt, or daylight showing through the rim joist is telling you something about February.

## Grading, Drainage, and the Melt

Snow eventually turns into a lot of water, all at once, usually in March. Walk the perimeter and look at whether the ground slopes away from the foundation or toward it. Look for efflorescence — that white mineral staining — on basement walls, and for a sump pump and whether it has a battery backup. A basement that's dry in September has not yet been tested.

## The One Nobody Asks About: Who Plows the Road?

Here's the question that gets skipped until the first storm. If the home is on a private or shared road — and a large share of homes outside the city limits of Kalispell, Whitefish, and Columbia Falls are — find out who plows it, under what agreement, and what it costs.

Ask for the road maintenance agreement in writing. Ask whether a homeowners' or road association collects for it, or whether it's a handshake between neighbors — and what happens if one owner stops paying. Ask how far the county plow actually comes. Then look at the driveway itself: length, slope, and whether there's anywhere to push the snow. A steep, shaded, north-facing driveway is a different winter than a flat one in the sun.

If the answer to all of this is a shrug, price a plow contract before you write the offer.

## How to Use This

None of this is a reason not to buy a house here. It's a reason to know what you're buying before you commit — and to write your inspection contingency with enough time to get a roofer or an HVAC tech out if the general inspection raises a flag. Most of these items come back as "fine" or "fixable for a known number." The point is to be the one who knows the number.

So: of the homes on your list right now, which one can you actually picture on a January morning — and which one have you only seen in September?
