# Why Divorcing Couples Should Use One Listing Agent

Somewhere in the first conversation about selling the house, someone usually asks a version of the same question: *should we each bring our own agent to this?*

It's a reasonable instinct. If the attorneys are separate, why wouldn't the agents be too?

Here's what I tell people, and why it's not the answer that makes me the most money: for a single marital home going to market, one neutral listing agent almost always serves both spouses better than two competing ones. Not because two agents can't be professional with each other — most can — but because of what the structure itself does to price, speed, and how much of this you have to relive.

## Two agents usually means dual agency problems or a diluted commission

There are really only two ways "each spouse has their own agent" plays out on a single listing, and neither is what people picture.

One version: both agents are technically working the same listing, splitting one commission, each reporting back to their own client. That's slower by design — every decision routes through two filters instead of one, and disagreements between the spouses get amplified by disagreements between their agents, who each have an incentive to look like they're advocating hard for their side.

The other version, more common than people expect: one spouse's "own agent" ends up representing the *buyer* instead, once it's clear there's only one listing to be had. Now you have one agent on the sale and one on the purchase side, which is a normal transaction structure — but it's not the protection either spouse thought they were getting when they made the call.

Neither version changes the sale price. The house is worth what it's worth. What changes is how much friction, delay, and duplicated explanation gets built into getting there.

## What a neutral agent actually does differently

A single agent working directly for both of you isn't a compromise position — it's a specific set of protocols, and I tell both spouses what they are before either signs anything.

**Same information, same time, always in writing.** Price opinions, showing feedback, offer summaries, inspection findings — one document, sent to both parties and both attorneys at once. No version of the numbers that one spouse sees first.

**No side conversations about strategy.** Either spouse can call me with a question about the process, and I'll answer it. What I won't do is take a call about how to get a better outcome *from* the other spouse. That's not neutrality with an asterisk — it's the whole point.

**Pricing gets set on the comps, not on who wants what number.** In an ordinary sale, a seller can chase a number for emotional reasons and it's their call to make. In a divorce sale, one spouse wanting to list high to "not leave money on the table" and the other wanting to list low to be done with it are both, in effect, asking the agent to take a side. Neither request moves the listing price. The comparable sales do.

**One person accountable for the whole file.** When something needs a decision — a repair request, a closing date that conflicts with one spouse's move, a buyer asking for a rent-back — there's one person who has the complete picture and gets it in front of both of you at once, instead of two agents each working from half the story.

## Where this actually saves money

The commission math is the most direct case for it. Splitting one listing's commission across two agents doesn't cost either spouse a discount — it just means two people are doing the coordination work that one person can do for the same total fee. That's not the strongest reason to do it, but it's a real one, and it's worth saying plainly since it's often the last thing anyone mentions.

The bigger cost is time. A stalled or mismanaged sale carries a mortgage payment, insurance, utilities, and property tax every month it drags — split, disputed, or simply unpaid while everyone waits for someone else to move first. A structure that gets to a clean closing faster is worth more than the commission split either way.

## What this doesn't decide

To be clear about the boundary: whether to sell at all, how the proceeds get divided, whether one spouse buys the other out instead — those are questions for your attorneys and, if it comes to it, the court. A neutral agent runs the listing well. A neutral agent doesn't decide what's fair, and I'm not going to pretend otherwise or offer legal or tax guidance I'm not licensed to give. Consult your attorney or CPA on anything touching the divorce settlement itself.

What a neutral agent *can* do is remove the real estate transaction as one more thing you're litigating through two separate lenses — so the parts that do need your attorneys get your attorneys' full attention, instead of competing with a listing dispute for it.

## A question worth asking before you each call an agent

If you pictured the sale finished and the keys handed over, would having gone through two competing agents have made that outcome better, or just made the six months before it harder?
